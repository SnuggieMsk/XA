# Chapter 17: Behavioural Finance in Practice — Short Notes

## 🎯 What you'll learn
- The **role of emotions in goal setting** (and how emotions sabotage money decisions)
- How to **"nudge" an investor** into behaving better
- The **role of the Investment Adviser** in managing client emotions

> **What is "Behavioural Finance"?** Normal (classical) finance assumes people are cold, calculating robots who always pick the option with the best numbers. **Behavioural finance** is the more honest version — it studies how real humans actually behave with money: emotionally, impulsively, and full of mental shortcuts called **biases** (built-in thinking errors). A good adviser's job is partly *financial* and partly *psychological*: protecting clients from their own brain.

## 17.1 Role of emotions in goal setting

People deal with the stress of modern life in different ways. Some overeat, some oversleep, some go on shopping sprees. In small doses this relieves stress, but it rarely stays small — and the **economic cost** (the money you wasted) eventually creates *more* stress.

**Analogy:** Emotional spending is like scratching a mosquito bite. The scratch feels great for two seconds, then the bite gets worse. The adviser's role is to put a self-limiting "brake" on these behaviours so they don't spiral.

Below are the specific emotional/behavioural traps the chapter covers, each with the **antidote** an adviser uses.

### (a) Retail therapy — stress buster or escape mechanism?
**Retail therapy** = going on a shopping/buying binge to reduce stress.

**How an adviser tackles it (the 2 steps):**
1. **Make the client conscious** that the spending is *triggered by stress*, not by genuine need.
2. **Set a specific budget** for such indulgence. In extreme cases, open a **separate account** holding only the budgeted amount; spend only from there (debit card or cash).

> 💡 The key idea is a **self-imposed brake** — a built-in expense limit. This is exactly why **credit cards are dangerous** for most people: they have no built-in brake and *induce impulsive spending* (you can spend money you don't have).

### (b) Too many and too frequent transactions
Nobel laureate **Paul Samuelson** said *"Investing should be dull... If you want excitement, take USD 800 and go to Las Vegas."*

Humans have an **action bias** = the urge to *do something* (anything!) rather than sit still. But investing rewards **patience and long-term consistency**. Action bias turns calm *investing* into hyperactive *trading* (frequent buying/selling), which usually destroys returns through costs and bad timing.

> 💡 **Antidote:** "Prime" (mentally prepare) the client *in advance* with the benefits of patience and long-term investing — before the itch to trade strikes.

### (c) Chasing past performance
Some investors just buy *last year's winners*: gold goes up → they pile into gold; equities rise → they switch to equities.

**Why it fails:** Most asset classes move in **cycles** of growth and decline, and there is an **inevitable reversion to the mean** (returns drift back toward the long-run average). Investing on past performance is **"like driving a car by looking in the rear-view mirror"** — you'll crash eventually.

> 💡 **Antidote:** A **pre-decided asset allocation policy** with **upper limits for each asset class** prevents over-exposure. A **maximum exposure limit within an asset class** does the same for any single security.

### (d) Home country bias
**Home country bias** = preferring to invest only in your *own country's* securities because they feel familiar (you know the companies, the rules). The cost: you lose the **diversification benefit** of owning assets that *don't move in tandem* with local markets.

Reasons investors avoid foreign assets:
- a) Lack of knowledge of opportunities abroad
- b) Home-country **laws may restrict** investing outside India
- c) Lack of access / high cost of access
- d) Complex **tax & legal compliance** in the other country (may conflict with local laws)
- e) Lack of expert advice (advisers themselves may not understand foreign assets)

**India context:** The **Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS)** lets a resident remit up to **USD 250,000** per year for overseas investment. Indian mutual funds also offer schemes investing abroad, including **S&P 500** and **Nasdaq 100** index funds.

> 💡 **Antidote:** The adviser must *get educated* on global investing and then *educate the client*.

### (e) Buying insurance only for tax saving
At least **3 generations of Indians** bought insurance *only* to save tax — and ended up with the **wrong** and/or **inadequate** insurance.

**The correct view:** Adequate, appropriate insurance is the **first step** of any financial plan. Tax saving merely *reduces the cost* of good insurance. In an investment-cum-insurance product it just *enhances return slightly* — which should still be compared to an equivalent tax-saving investment.

> 💡 **Worked logic:** Buying a **pure term policy + investing in PPF** will *invariably beat* buying an **endowment** (investment-cum-insurance) policy for the same outlay. The cure: highlight the need for **adequate pure insurance** and the **poor returns** of bundled insurance-investment products.

### (f) Too much diversification OR a highly concentrated portfolio
Two opposite mistakes:
- **Over-diversification:** Owning so many securities you can't manage them. Buying 2 large-cap + 4 mid-cap + 6 small-cap funds is pointless — you could get the **same diversification by buying a single top-500 index fund**. Diversification doesn't come from *number* of securities.
- **Over-concentration:** Putting your whole portfolio in one or two securities/assets with no spread.

> 💡 The adviser finds the **"golden mean"** — appropriate diversification based on the client's **risk profile, resources, and time frame**.

### (g) The impact of framing on risk-tolerance questions
**Framing** = *how* you word the same fact. One client responds to *"here's what you'll gain"*; another responds to *"here's what you'll lose by not doing this."* Both descriptions justify the *same* recommendation — but the **framing decides whether the client is convinced.** Tools exist that diagnose which framing a client prefers; confirm the diagnosis through discussion.

### (h) Overconfidence and dilution in risk management
Humans **overestimate their own abilities**. Survey employees on what performance percentile they're in: almost everyone says 60–75th, almost no one says below 50th — which is **mathematically impossible**. People simply think they're better than they are.

In investing, this shows up as **attributing luck to skill**: a client made a small bet on a stock that 5× in 3 months. That was *mostly luck*, and even if skill, it's unlikely to be repeatable. Overconfident, the client may **over-invest** and wreck their asset allocation.

> ⚠️ This is when *"the Investment Adviser's influence can stand between the client and her big mistake."*

## 17.2 Nudging the investor to behave better

**A "nudge"** = a gentle, pre-built push that makes the *good* choice the *easy/automatic* choice — without forcing anyone.

**How:** Lay down **advance ground rules** that the client agrees to up front. Then the rules act as automatic nudges.

> 💡 **Example:** Ground rules on **when to rebalance** become a built-in nudge to **book profit when prices are high** (sell the asset that grew too big) and **buy when prices are low** (top up the asset that shrank). The rule forces "buy low, sell high" — the opposite of what emotions tell you to do.

## 17.3 Role of the Investment Adviser in managing client emotions

Most clients ride a cycle of **greed and fear**:
- **Over-exuberance (greed)** → rash decisions
- **Overarching fear** → paralysis (the client "freezes")

In both cases the adviser is the **cool, calming influence** that keeps the client on the *pre-planned journey* through the market's ups and downs.

**Second role — prioritising goals when money is short.** Example: a client must fund (i) her daughter's higher education and (ii) her own retirement. Emotionally she'll sacrifice retirement (it feels far away) for the daughter's best education. The adviser objectively:
- Points out **how much the vital retirement goal is being compromised**, and
- Offers **alternatives** — e.g. a **partial education loan** the daughter repays from her own future earnings.

> 💡 This **objective role** helps highly emotional clients make the *right* decision.

## 📌 One-Page Recap
- **Behavioural finance** studies how real (emotional, biased) humans handle money — vs the robot-investor of classical finance.
- **Emotional/behavioural traps & antidotes:**
  - *Retail therapy* → make it conscious + separate budgeted account (no credit cards).
  - *Action bias / over-trading* → prime the client on patience; investing should be "dull."
  - *Chasing past performance* → pre-set asset-allocation limits; beware reversion to the mean.
  - *Home country bias* → educate on global investing; LRS allows USD 250k/yr.
  - *Insurance for tax only* → buy *adequate pure* insurance; term + PPF beats endowment.
  - *Over/under-diversification* → an index fund gives diversification; find the golden mean.
  - *Framing* → word the recommendation the way the client responds to (gain vs loss).
  - *Overconfidence* → don't mistake luck for skill; protect the asset allocation.
- **Nudges:** pre-agreed ground rules (e.g. rebalancing) make good behaviour automatic.
- **Managing emotions:** be the calm anchor through greed/fear cycles; help prioritise goals objectively (education loan vs raiding retirement).
- **Theory names (from module questions):** *Prospect Theory* and *Mental Accounting* are core behavioural concepts.

## 🔑 Key Numbers & Terms
| Term / Number | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Behavioural finance** | Study of how psychology/emotion affects real money decisions (includes investor psychology). |
| **Action bias** | The urge to "do something," causing over-trading. |
| **Reversion to the mean** | Returns tend to drift back to the long-run average — why chasing winners fails. |
| **Home country bias** | Investing only in domestic securities, losing diversification. |
| **LRS limit** | **USD 250,000** per resident per year for overseas investment. |
| **Framing** | How a fact is worded (gain-frame vs loss-frame) changes the decision. |
| **Overconfidence bias** | Overestimating one's own skill; mistaking luck for skill. |
| **Nudge** | A pre-built gentle push (e.g. ground rules) toward good behaviour. |
| **Anchoring bias** | Relying on pre-existing information when making decisions. |
| **Mental Accounting** | Treating money differently based on its origin/intended use. |
| **Prospect Theory** | Kahneman & Tversky (1979): choices judged vs a reference point; losses hurt more than equal gains; risk-averse on gains, risk-seeking on losses. |
