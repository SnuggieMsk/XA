# Chapter 17: Behavioural Finance in Practice — Flashcards

> **How to use:** Cover the answer, recall it out loud, then check.

**Q1.** What is behavioural finance, and how does it differ from standard finance?
> **A:** It is the study of how human psychology and emotion affect money decisions. Standard finance assumes rational "robot" investors; behavioural finance **includes the impact of investor psychology** (and accepts that market anomalies exist).

**Q2.** What is "retail therapy"?
> **A:** Going on a shopping/buying binge to relieve emotional stress.

**Q3.** What are the two steps an adviser uses to control retail therapy?
> **A:** (1) Make the client **conscious** that the spending is stress-induced; (2) Set a **specific budget** — in extreme cases a **separate account** holding only the budgeted amount, spent via debit card/cash.

**Q4.** Why are credit cards considered bad for most people in this context?
> **A:** They have **no built-in expense brake** and **induce impulsive spending** (you can spend money you don't have).

**Q5.** What is "action bias"?
> **A:** The human need to *do something* (constantly act), which conflicts with investing's need for long-term patience and turns investing into frequent **trading**.

**Q6.** Paul Samuelson said investing should be like what?
> **A:** "Dull... like watching paint dry or grass grow." For excitement, "take USD 800 and go to Las Vegas."

**Q7.** How does an adviser curb the over-trading tendency?
> **A:** **Prime the client in advance** with the benefits of patience and long-term investing.

**Q8.** What is "chasing past performance" and why is it dangerous?
> **A:** Investing in last year's winners, assuming they'll repeat. Dangerous because asset classes move in cycles with **reversion to the mean** — "like driving by looking in the rear-view mirror."

**Q9.** What policy tool prevents chasing performance / over-exposure?
> **A:** A **pre-decided asset allocation policy** with **upper limits per asset class**, plus a **maximum exposure limit within an asset class** for any single security.

**Q10.** What is "home country bias"?
> **A:** Preferring to invest only in your home country's securities due to familiarity, thereby losing the diversification benefit of low/negatively-correlated foreign assets.

**Q11.** List reasons investors avoid foreign securities.
> **A:** Lack of knowledge; home-country laws restricting it; lack of access / high cost; complex foreign tax & legal compliance; lack of expert advice.

**Q12.** What is the LRS and its limit?
> **A:** **Liberalised Remittance Scheme** — a resident may remit up to **USD 250,000 per year** for overseas investment.

**Q13.** Name two global indices available as Indian mutual fund schemes.
> **A:** **S&P 500** and **Nasdaq 100**.

**Q14.** What is the antidote to home country bias?
> **A:** The adviser educates *themselves* on global opportunities, then educates the client.

**Q15.** Why is "buying insurance only for tax saving" a problem?
> **A:** Clients end up with the **wrong** and/or **inadequate** insurance. Adequate pure insurance should come first; tax saving only reduces its cost.

**Q16.** Which combination beats an endowment policy for the same outlay?
> **A:** A **pure term policy + investing in PPF** invariably beats an endowment (investment-cum-insurance) plan.

**Q17.** Why is owning many funds NOT the same as diversification?
> **A:** Diversification comes from spread, not count. 2 large-cap + 4 mid-cap + 6 small-cap funds give roughly the same diversification as a single **top-500 index fund**.

**Q18.** What two opposite portfolio mistakes does the chapter warn about?
> **A:** **Over-diversification** (too many unmanageable holdings) and **over-concentration** (whole portfolio in one or two securities/assets).

**Q19.** What is "framing"?
> **A:** *How* the same fact is worded. Some clients respond to a **gain frame** ("what you'll gain"), others to a **loss frame** ("what you lose by not doing it"). Same recommendation, different persuasion.

**Q20.** What is overconfidence bias, with the survey example?
> **A:** Overestimating one's abilities. Most employees rate themselves in the 60–75th percentile — an arithmetical impossibility. In investing it means mistaking **luck for skill**.

**Q21.** What's the danger of overconfidence in investing, and the adviser's role?
> **A:** The client over-invests on a lucky bet, distorting the asset allocation. The adviser stands "between the client and her big mistake."

**Q22.** What is a "nudge" in investing?
> **A:** A pre-built gentle push (e.g. agreed **ground rules**) that makes the good choice automatic without forcing it.

**Q23.** Give the classic nudge example.
> **A:** **Rebalancing ground rules** — they auto-trigger profit-booking when prices are high and buying when prices are low ("buy low, sell high").

**Q24.** What emotional cycle do most clients go through?
> **A:** The **greed/fear cycle** — over-exuberance leading to rash acts, or fear that freezes them.

**Q25.** What is the adviser's role during the greed/fear cycle?
> **A:** Be the **cool, calming influence** that keeps the client on the pre-planned journey.

**Q26.** How does the adviser help when resources can't fund all goals (education vs retirement)?
> **A:** Objectively show how much the vital retirement goal is being compromised and offer alternatives, e.g. a **partial education loan** the child repays later.

**Q27.** What is anchoring bias?
> **A:** Relying on **pre-existing information** when making decisions.

**Q28.** What does mental accounting theory say?
> **A:** People **treat money differently** depending on its **origin and intended use**.

**Q29.** Who introduced Prospect Theory and when?
> **A:** **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979).**

**Q30.** State the three premises of Prospect Theory.
> **A:** (1) Choices are evaluated relative to a **reference point**; (2) People are **risk-averse on gains** (realise them early) but **risk-seeking on losses** (hold them longer); (3) **Losses hurt more than equal gains**.
