# Chapter 18: Risk Profiling for Investors — Short Notes

## 🎯 What you'll learn
- What **risk profiling** is and the approach to doing it.
- The **parameters** (factors) that affect how much risk a person can/should take.
- How risk profiling drives **asset allocation** (Strategic, Tactical, Dynamic), model portfolios, and rebalancing.

> **Three words you must never mix up:**
> - **Risk appetite / willingness** = how much risk a person *wants* to take (psychological — their attitude to volatility and losses).
> - **Risk capacity / ability** = how much risk a person *can financially afford* to take (their resources — income, savings, dependents).
> - **Risk tolerance** = the level of risk a client is *willing to accept*, measured by a questionnaire. It blends willingness and ability.
>
> A young person may be *willing* to take big risk (high appetite) but if they have huge loans and many dependents, their *ability* is low. Good profiling looks at **both**.

## 18.1 Risk profiling and the risk profiling approach

### The coin-toss example (understand "willingness")
You pay **Rs. 1,00,000** to toss a coin. Call heads/tails; if right, you win **Rs. 2,10,000**.

Let's compute the **expected (average) payoff**:
- If you **guess right** → net gain = 2,10,000 − 1,00,000 = **Rs. 1,10,000**. At 50% chance → expected win = **Rs. 55,000**.
- If you **guess wrong** → you lose your stake = **Rs. 1,00,000**. At 50% chance → expected loss = **Rs. 50,000**.
- **Total expected payoff = 55,000 − 50,000 = Rs. 5,000** (positive — statistically a "good" bet).

Even though the maths is favourable, **most people refuse** — the **fear of losing Rs. 1,00,000** is too strong. More people agree only when the prize rises to Rs. 2,50,000 or Rs. 3,00,000.

**Lesson:** **Willingness to take risk is about psychology, not just the numbers.** Some people find volatility and possible losses distressing; others are relaxed about it.

💡 Whether the risk is *actually* taken also depends on:
- **Resources** — more resources → more likely to take the risk (higher *ability*).
- **Need** — the more important the payoff is, the more likely they take the risk.

### Factors affecting risk appetite (Table 18.1)
| Factor | Effect on risk appetite (all else equal) |
|---|---|
| **Earning members** | More earning members → **higher** risk appetite |
| **Dependent members** | More dependents → **lower** risk appetite |
| **Life expectancy** | Longer life expectancy → **higher** risk appetite |
| **Age** | Lower age → **higher** risk can be taken |
| **Employability** | Well-qualified, multi-skilled → can take **more** risk |
| **Nature of job** | Steady job → can take **more** risk |
| **Knowledge of markets** | Better informed → can take **more** market risk |
| **Psyche** | Daring/adventurous → mentally accept downsides better |
| **Capital base** | Higher capital → better able to absorb losses (**more** risk) |
| **Regularity of income** | Regular income → can take **more** risk than irregular income |

**Analogy:** Risk appetite is like how spicy a meal you'll order. *Willingness* = how much you like spice. *Ability* = whether your stomach can handle it. Both matter before you order.

### What a risk profile measures
A client's **financial risk tolerance** — attitudes, values, motivations, preferences, experiences — is captured in a **risk profile**, typically via a **questionnaire**. Responses become a **score** that classifies the client into a category.

## 18.2 Parameters for risk profiling

Online risk-profiling tools ask a few questions and generate a score. **Limitations:**
- Investors may try to **"guess the right answer"** — but there is **no right answer**. The tool only works if the investor is **truthful**.
- Some advanced profilers use **scenario responses**, or assess profile from a client's **actual transaction history**.
- Tools vary in **robustness**; the adviser must check this and use them **judiciously**.

### The 3-grade classification
| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **I. Conservative** | Dislikes risk; new to risky instruments; prefers bank accounts / safe income instruments; *may* invest a small portion in risky assets for the long term. |
| **II. Moderate** | Some investment experience (including equities); understands risk is needed for long-term goals; willing to risk **part** of assets. |
| **III. Aggressive** | Experienced; used a range of products; active manager; understands risk is crucial for long-term returns; willing to risk a **significant portion** of assets. |

### SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulation 16 — what the IA MUST do
The IA must ensure risk profiling is done so the advice/product is **suitable**. Specifically:
1. **Obtain relevant information** — age; investment objectives & time frames; resources (income, existing investments, innate ability to take risks, loan details).
2. Have an **objective process** to assess the client's **willingness AND ability** to take risk (including ability to **incur and absorb losses**) — not slanted to over/under-weight certain factors.
3. Any **tools/questionnaires** must be **fit for purpose**; limitations identified and mitigated.
4. Questionnaires must be **fair, clear, not misleading**, not vague, not in difficult language, and contain **no leading questions**.
5. Risk profile must be done **before any advice**. **No free trial** without communicating the risk profile to the client and getting their **consent** on it.
6. Risk profile must be **updated periodically** — recommended on **special events**: change in family composition, income/expenses, or assets/liabilities.

⚠️ Trap: "No free trial without communicating the risk profile and obtaining consent" — easy exam point.

## 18.3 Role of risk profiling in asset allocation

> **What is "asset allocation"?** Dividing your portfolio among different **asset classes** (equity, debt, gold, real estate, cash) so a fall in one is cushioned by others. *"Don't put all your eggs in one basket."*

**Why it works:** Asset classes are driven by **different factors** and don't move together. Example: in the **2007–09 recession**, equities fell in many countries but **gold rose** — an investor in both did better than one in equities alone. An **efficient asset allocation** combines assets with **low or negative correlation** (they don't rise and fall together).

💡 International research suggests **asset allocation and investment policy explain portfolio performance better than stock selection** (picking individual securities).

### The three types of asset allocation
| Type | What it is | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategic** | Allocation **aligned to financial goals** | Based on required return, time horizon, and risk profile. A **long-term plan**, reviewed periodically. Allocation changes only if **fundamentals** change — NOT based on expected asset performance. Rebalanced periodically back to the original mix. Protected from forecast errors, but won't benefit from over-weighting a hot asset. |
| **Tactical** | A **call on the likely behaviour of the market** | E.g., going **overweight equities** because you expect a market rally. Only for **seasoned investors with large surpluses**; even then, applied to a **limited portion**; the bulk stays strategic. |
| **Dynamic** | Uses **pre-defined models** to allocate | Triggers (asset valuations or portfolio performance) decide reallocation. **Removes the subjective element.** |

💡 Exam favourite: **Tactical asset allocation depends on the market**; strategic does not.

### 18.3.2 Model Portfolios
Since needs vary, no single portfolio fits all. Advisers use **model portfolios** — the most appropriate asset mix for each profile, then tweak per the individual.

| Client profile | Model allocation (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| **Young BPO employee, no dependents (high risk)** | 50% equities/diversified equity (via SIP); 20% sector funds; 10% gold ETF; 10% diversified debt/FD; 10% short-term debt & liquid/savings |
| **Young married, single income, 2 school kids (moderate)** | 35% equities; 10% sector funds; 15% gold ETF; 30% diversified debt/FD; 10% liquid & short-term debt/savings |
| **Single income, grown-up children not yet settled (conservative)** | 35% equities; 15% gold ETF; 10% gilt fund; 20% diversified debt/FD; 20% short-term debt & liquid/savings |
| **Couple in their 70s, no family support (conservative–moderate)** | 15% equity index; 5% gold ETF; 35% debt-oriented hybrid/MIS/SCSS; 30% diversified debt/FD; 15% liquid/savings |

> 💡 These percentages are **illustrative and highly subjective**. The key point: have a model portfolio for each distinct profile, then tweak. (A physically/mentally sound 70s couple with a large corpus might take slightly more equity than the standard 70s model.)

### Portfolio construction — matching objectives to investments
No single investment gives growth + liquidity + income + capital protection + good return. Build a portfolio:

| Objective | Suitable investments |
|---|---|
| **Growth / appreciation** | Equity shares & equity funds, real estate, gold |
| **Regular income** | Deposits, debt instruments & debt funds, real estate |
| **Liquidity** | Cash, bank deposits, short-term MF schemes |
| **Capital preservation** | Cash, bank deposits, ultra-short-term funds |

**Concentration warnings (from the chapter):**
- **Jayesh** (only real estate) → can't sell/borrow quickly when he needs cash → **no liquidity**.
- **Kamal** (all in savings account) → very low interest → **can't accumulate enough**.
- **Latika** (all equities) → value keeps fluctuating → **uncertain** funds when needed.
- **Harmeet** (mostly gold jewellery) → **no income** for monthly expenses.
- **Gayatri** (all bank FDs) → fixed interest but **rising expenses** → income can't keep up.

The common risk = **concentration** in one category. Spreading across assets cushions a fall in any one. A person near retirement → more debt, less equity; a high-earner with far-off goals → more equity. Suitable allocation depends on **investment period + ability + willingness** to take risk.

### Review and Rebalancing
**Review** the portfolio periodically (ideally at least **once a year**) and ask:
- Are all goals still relevant?
- Are goals on target in the required time-frame?
- Are investments performing as expected?
- Do investments need changing if no longer suitable?

If a goal is under-funded, the investor can **save more** or **divert funds from a less important goal**.

**Rebalancing** = modifying exposure to different asset classes so the portfolio stays aligned to the investor's risk/return needs — **not** to chase short-term price moves.

💡 **Example (Jayant):** Saved for kids' education in **equity** for 8 years; goal is now **4 years away**. To avoid equity fluctuations damaging the accumulated amount, he **moves to less risky investments** as the goal nears. (Lesson: shift from growth to safety as a goal approaches.)

## 📌 One-Page Recap
- **Willingness** (psychology) vs **ability/capacity** (resources) vs **tolerance** (measured, blends both).
- Coin-toss: even a statistically good bet is refused due to **fear of loss** → willingness is psychological.
- Factors ↑ appetite: more earners, longer life expectancy, lower age, employability, steady job, market knowledge, daring psyche, high capital, regular income. **More dependents ↓ appetite.**
- 3 grades: **Conservative / Moderate / Aggressive.**
- **SEBI Reg 16:** objective profiling (willingness + ability), fit-for-purpose & non-leading questionnaires, profile **before advice**, **no free trial without consent**, update on life events.
- Asset allocation: combine **low/negative-correlation** assets. Allocation matters more than stock selection.
- **Strategic** (goal-aligned, long-term, doesn't chase performance) / **Tactical** (market call, seasoned investors, limited portion) / **Dynamic** (model-driven, no subjectivity).
- **Model portfolios** per profile; tweak per individual.
- Match objective → investment (growth/income/liquidity/preservation). Avoid **concentration**.
- **Review ≥ once a year**; **rebalance** to stay aligned (not to chase prices); shift from growth to safety as a goal nears.
