## 16.1 Behavioural Finance vs Standard Finance

> **Behavioural Finance** = the study of how **psychology** influences the behaviour of market participants (individuals and groups), and the effect on financial markets. It's part of **Behavioural Economics**.

It tries to explain the **gaps and market anomalies** that standard finance can't.

**Standard finance** assumes investors are perfectly logical robots. Its key assumptions:
- Investors are **rational**
- Investors are **risk averse**
- Investors are **self-interested utility maximizers** (always chase the best outcome)
- Investors **update their beliefs** as new information arrives
- Investors have **access to all information**

**But in real life,** people behave very differently:
- They hold **concentrated** portfolios instead of diversifying.
- They swing between **greed and fear**.
- They credit **luck to their own skill**.
- They confuse a **good company** with a **good stock**.
- They prefer **domestic companies** (**home bias**) because familiarity feels safer.

**Analogy:** Standard finance is like assuming every driver obeys every traffic rule perfectly with full information. Behavioural finance studies how drivers *actually* behave — speeding when excited, freezing when scared, following the car ahead.

| **Standard Finance** | **Behavioural Finance** |
|---|---|
| **Economics** at its core | **Psychology** at its core |
| Investors rational, process info without bias; efficient markets, random price movement | **Biases** guide decisions; herd mentality drives sharp moves |
| Decision-making is rule-driven & consistent | Decision-making is **inconsistent** (experience, recency, loss aversion) |
| Risk-return trade-off is the foundation | **Loss aversion** is the basis — over-sensitive to losses |
| Rational, detailed analysis | Ad-hoc, **thumb-rule** based |

💡 Nobel laureates **Daniel Kahneman (2002)** and **Richard Thaler (2017)** brought behavioural finance to the forefront.

## 16.2 How do individuals make decisions?

Real investors show **limits to rationality** — they don't act like the rational robots of standard theory.

### 16.2.1 Bounded Rationality
> **Bounded Rationality** = the mind's limitation where, lacking time or full information, people settle for a **"satisficing"** solution (satisfactory + sufficient) instead of the **optimal** one.

People **"satisfice"** rather than optimize. **Important:** this is **NOT irrational** — it's rational decision-making *under boundary conditions* (limited time/info/ability).

**Analogy:** Choosing lunch from a 100-item menu, you don't analyse all 100 dishes — you pick something that looks "good enough" quickly. That's satisficing.

The steps (per **Herbert Simon, 1978**):
1. Process only the **manageable** information (not all of it)
2. Apply **rules of thumb / quick approaches**
3. Pick a **satisfactory and sufficient** (satisficing) solution

Bounded rationality sits **between** pure **rationality** (process all info → optimal) and **intuition** (pure experience).

### 16.2.2 Prospect Theory
Introduced by **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979)**. It describes how people choose between **risky alternatives** and how they value gains and losses.

**Two phases:**
1. **Framing phase** — prospects are framed (using heuristics for a quick preliminary analysis)
2. **Evaluation phase** — the edited prospects are evaluated and the highest-perceived-value one is chosen

**Three premises (memorise!):**
- Choices are judged **relative to a reference point** (your current well-being), not in absolute terms.
- People are **risk-averse about gains** (they grab gains early) but **risk-seeking about losses** (they hold losers longer, hoping to break even).
- **Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good.**

**Analogy:** Finding ₹500 feels nice; **losing** ₹500 feels much worse. The pain outweighs the pleasure even though the amount is the same.

## 16.3 Categorization of Biases

> **Bias** = processing information based on personal experiences/preferences, leading to systematic errors/favouring.

Biases fall into **two main categories**:
- **Emotional biases** — based on **feelings/emotions**
- **Cognitive errors** — based on **faulty reasoning/thinking**

💡 You can't be fully unbiased, but **discipline and checklists** help mitigate biases.

### 16.3.1 Emotional Biases
These arise spontaneously from deep-rooted personal experiences. They aren't always "errors" — often they're **protective** (choosing what feels comfortable/safe).

| Emotional Bias | What it is | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| **Loss Aversion** | People take chances to *avoid losses* but won't gamble with sure gains; losses are far more powerful than gains | An investor refuses to sell a falling stock (hoping to avoid "booking a loss") but quickly sells a rising one. **Disposition Effect** (Shefrin & Statman, 1985): holding **losers too long**, selling **winners too early** |
| **Stereotyping** | Judging based on representative characteristics / general perception | Believing a **high-profile manager** automatically = a well-run company = a good investment |
| **Overconfidence** | Unwarranted faith in one's own reasoning/abilities; most rate themselves "above average" | Trading excessively, concentrating the portfolio, confusing **a bull market with personal skill** (worsened by **self-attribution bias** — "I'm a genius"). Blurs skill vs luck |
| **Endowment** | Valuing an asset more *just because you own it* | Refusing to sell **inherited stocks** even if they don't fit the strategy (combines valuing ownership + loss aversion) |
| **Status Quo** | Doing nothing / sticking with the default to avoid regret | Never switching a poor mutual fund because changing "feels risky." Linked to **Regret Aversion** |

**Regret has two dimensions:**
- **Error of commission** — regret over **actions you took**
- **Error of omission** — regret over **actions you didn't take**

💡 Regret from **commission** feels worse than from **omission**, so people prefer the status quo. A simple **"nudge"** (Richard Thaler) — like auto-enrolling employees into a pension plan — overcomes this and dramatically boosts enrolment.

### 16.3.2 Cognitive Errors
> **Cognitive errors** = statistical, information-processing, or memory errors causing deviation from rational behaviour. They come from **faulty thinking** (vs emotional biases from **feelings**).

These are **short-cuts** to avoid the pain of full analysis — and being **vigilant** reduces them.

| Cognitive Error | What it is | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mental Accounting** | Treating equal sums of money differently based on which mental "bucket" they're in (money isn't truly **fungible** to them) | Spending a **bonus or lottery win** recklessly but being careful with salary; keeping separate "retirement," "education," "marriage" buckets and over-managing each instead of optimizing the whole portfolio. (**Thaler, 1999**) |
| **Framing** | Answering differently based on **how a question is phrased** | "**95% lean**" beef sounds better than "**5% fat**" — identical fact, different choice. Affects how investors fill **risk-tolerance questionnaires** |
| **Anchoring** | Over-relying on an initial piece of information (the "anchor") | The **first price** mentioned in a negotiation anchors the whole deal; judging a stock's worth purely by its **past price** |
| **Choice Paralysis** | Too many options (or too much info) → freezing and not deciding at all | Faced with 50 mutual funds, the investor keeps "researching" and never invests |

> **Fungibility** = the fact that all money is interchangeable and has no labels. Thaler advised: treat all money **the same**, regardless of origin or use.

💡 Distinction to remember: **emotional bias = from feelings (protective)**; **cognitive error = from faulty thinking/shortcuts (factually wrong, fixable with vigilance).**

## 16.4 Fusion Investing

> **Fusion Investing** = combining **fundamental analysis** (traditional) with **behavioural finance** to build investment strategies.

- **Fundamental view:** a stock's price = the discounted value of future cash flows; all info is reflected in price (long-term "weighing machine").
- **Behavioural view:** short-term prices are volatile, driven by the collective behaviour of investors/traders (short-term "voting machine").

**Benjamin Graham:** *"In the short run the market is a voting machine, and in the long run it is a weighing machine."* Fusion investing integrates **both** the voting (momentum) and weighing (value) machines — combining the **value-growth** phenomenon and the **momentum effect**.

**Three steps to pick fusion stocks:**
1. **Step I** — Find stocks looking promising on **value** (P/E, P/BV, P/S).
2. **Step II** — Filter those that are **fundamentally strong** (profitability, leverage, efficiency — e.g. **Piotroski Score**, 0–9, where 9 is best).
3. **Step III** — From those, pick stocks showing **strong momentum** (price uptrend).

⚠️ Momentum (Step III) is **short-term**, so it needs **frequent rebalancing** → higher **transaction costs** and **tax** to consider. Market data hasn't conclusively proven the strategy works.

## 16.5 Behavioural Finance Explains Market Anomalies

> **Market anomaly** = a departure of a stock's price from its expected value (per CAPM/asset pricing models) that can't be explained by known/new information.

Anomalies appear, disappear, and reappear randomly. They *should* vanish as people exploit them — but some persist.

**How biases cause anomalies:**
- **Overconfidence** → underestimating risk.
- **Self-attribution** → taking credit for wins, blaming others for losses; following only confirming info → **over-trading**.
- **Disposition effect** (hold losers, sell winners) → explains **price momentum**.

## 16.6 Behavioural Finance Explains Bubbles and Crashes

**Charles Kindleberger** (*Manias, Panics and Crashes*, 1978): a **bubble** = a sharp, continuous rise in asset price, where the initial rise creates expectations of *further* rises, attracting new buyers (speculators chasing trading profit, not long-term value). *Psychology is as important as economics.*

⚠️ Why don't smart **arbitrageurs** fix mispricing? They face a **synchronisation problem** (can't coordinate selling) and differing incentives to time the market — so bubbles **persist**.

**Famous bubbles:** Dutch Tulip mania (1630s), South Sea Bubble (1720s), Tech bubble (2000s). Even **Isaac Newton** lost ~£20,000 in the South Sea Bubble, saying *"I can calculate the motions of heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people."*

**Pattern:** Bubbles often start with a *justifiable* price rise (new product/disruption/productivity), changing future expectations. Early investors earn huge returns, attracting more; **cheap borrowing** fuels it; analysts invent **"new age" theories** to justify crazy prices.

**Reasons bubbles form:**
- **Liquidity** — cheap money chasing stocks lifts prices in a vicious cycle.
- **Celebrity status** — media hype is self-fulfilling, disconnecting price from value.
- **Momentum** — investors extrapolate trends → widespread **herd trading** overwhelms rational investors.
- **Illusion of Control** — familiarity/involvement makes investors *believe* they can forecast and sell before everyone else.

**Keith Redhead (2008)** — bubbles are more likely when:
- A high proportion of **inexperienced traders**
- High **uncertainty** about true value
- Small chance of profit but **very high** potential profit
- Purchases can be financed by **borrowing**
- **Short selling is difficult**

**Crashes** = the same processes **in reverse** — everyone rushes to sell at once (negative news, opinion change, liquidity crunch, return to fundamentals), and the jostling to exit first causes the crash.

## 📌 One-Page Recap
- **Standard finance** = rational robots, economics core; **Behavioural finance** = real psychology, biases, herd behaviour.
- **Bounded rationality** = "satisficing" (good enough) under limited time/info — rational, not irrational (Herbert Simon).
- **Prospect theory** (Kahneman & Tversky): choices vs a **reference point**; **risk-averse on gains, risk-seeking on losses**; **losses hurt more than gains please**.
- **Emotional biases** (from feelings): Loss Aversion (Disposition Effect), Stereotyping, Overconfidence (+ self-attribution), Endowment, Status Quo (+ regret aversion).
- **Cognitive errors** (from faulty thinking): Mental Accounting, Framing, Anchoring, Choice Paralysis.
- **Regret:** commission (action) hurts more than omission (inaction) → status quo. A **nudge** fixes it.
- **Fusion investing** = value (weighing machine) + momentum (voting machine); steps: value → fundamentals → momentum.
- Biases create **anomalies, bubbles, and crashes**; arbitrageurs can't always correct them (synchronisation problem).
