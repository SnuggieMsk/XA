# Chapter 16: Basics of Behavioural Finance — Flashcards

Cover the answer, recall it, then check.

**Q1.** What is Behavioural Finance?
> **A:** The study of how **psychology** influences the behaviour of market participants (individuals and groups) and its effect on financial markets. It's a part of Behavioural Economics and explains gaps/anomalies that standard finance can't.

**Q2.** What are the five key assumptions of Standard Finance?
> **A:** Investors are (1) **rational**, (2) **risk averse**, (3) **self-interested utility maximizers**, (4) **update beliefs** with new information, and (5) have **access to all information**.

**Q3.** Give three real-life behaviours that contradict standard finance assumptions.
> **A:** Holding **concentrated** portfolios instead of diversifying; swinging between **greed and fear**; crediting **luck to skill**; confusing a good company with a good stock; **home bias** (preferring domestic firms).

**Q4.** At its core, Standard Finance is based on ___, while Behavioural Finance is based on ___.
> **A:** **Economics**; **Psychology**.

**Q5.** Which two Nobel laureates are credited with bringing Behavioural Finance forward?
> **A:** **Daniel Kahneman (2002)** and **Richard Thaler (2017)**.

**Q6.** What is Bounded Rationality?
> **A:** The mind's cognitive limitation where, lacking time/information, people choose a **satisficing** (satisfactory + sufficient) solution instead of the optimal one. It's rational decision-making under boundary conditions — **not irrational**.

**Q7.** What does "satisfice" mean and who coined Bounded Rationality?
> **A:** "Satisfice" = picking an option that's **satisfactory and sufficient** (good enough), not optimal. **Herbert Simon (1978)**.

**Q8.** What are the three steps of decision-making under Bounded Rationality?
> **A:** (1) Process only **manageable** information; (2) apply **rules of thumb / quick approaches**; (3) select a **satisficing** solution.

**Q9.** Who introduced Prospect Theory and when?
> **A:** **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979)**.

**Q10.** What are the two phases of choice in Prospect Theory?
> **A:** (1) A **framing** phase (prospects are framed using heuristics) and (2) an **evaluation** phase (the edited prospect of highest perceived value is chosen).

**Q11.** State the three premises of Prospect Theory.
> **A:** (1) Choices are evaluated relative to a **reference point** (well-being); (2) people are **risk-averse about gains** but **risk-seeking about losses**; (3) **losses hurt more than gains feel good**.

**Q12.** What are the two broad categories of biases?
> **A:** **Emotional biases** (based on feelings) and **Cognitive errors** (based on faulty thinking/reasoning).

**Q13.** What is Loss Aversion bias?
> **A:** People take chances to **avoid losses** but won't gamble with sure gains; losses are far more powerful than equal gains.

**Q14.** What is the Disposition Effect?
> **A:** Proposed by Shefrin & Statman (1985): investors **hold losers too long** and **sell winners too early** — they dislike booking losses and are eager to lock in gains.

**Q15.** What is Stereotyping bias?
> **A:** Basing decisions on representative characteristics / general perceptions — e.g. assuming a **high-profile manager** means a well-managed company and good investment.

**Q16.** What is Overconfidence bias, and what worsens it?
> **A:** Unwarranted faith in one's own reasoning/abilities (most rate themselves above average). It's intensified by **self-attribution bias** (confusing a bull market / luck with personal skill) and leads to excessive trading and portfolio concentration.

**Q17.** What is Endowment bias?
> **A:** Valuing an asset more **just because you own it**. It combines valuing ownership + loss aversion — e.g. rarely selling **inherited stocks** even if they don't fit the strategy.

**Q18.** What is Status Quo bias and what is it linked to?
> **A:** Taking **no decision** / sticking with the default to avoid regret. It's closely linked to **Regret Aversion bias**.

**Q19.** What are the two dimensions of regret, and which hurts more?
> **A:** **Error of commission** (regret over actions taken) and **error of omission** (regret over actions not taken). **Commission** hurts more, so people prefer the status quo.

**Q20.** How does a "nudge" relate to status quo bias?
> **A:** Richard Thaler showed a simple nudge (e.g. **auto-enrolling** employees into a pension plan) overcomes status-quo inertia and dramatically improves enrolment.

**Q21.** How do cognitive errors differ from emotional biases?
> **A:** Cognitive errors come from **faulty thinking** (statistical/info-processing/memory errors) and can be factually wrong and reduced by vigilance; emotional biases come from **feelings** and are often protective.

**Q22.** What is Mental Accounting?
> **A:** Treating equal sums of money differently based on which mental "bucket" they're in — e.g. spending a **bonus/lottery** recklessly but salary carefully. Developed by **Thaler (1999)**.

**Q23.** What is fungibility, and what did Thaler recommend about it?
> **A:** **Fungibility** = all money is interchangeable and has no labels. Thaler recommended treating **all money the same**, regardless of origin or use.

**Q24.** What is Framing bias? Give an example.
> **A:** Answering differently based on **how a question is phrased** — e.g. "95% lean" beef vs "5% fat" beef (same fact, different choice). It affects risk-tolerance questionnaires.

**Q25.** What is Anchoring bias?
> **A:** Over-relying on an **initial piece of information** (the anchor) so all later info is judged in its light — e.g. the first price in a negotiation, or judging a stock by its past price.

**Q26.** What is Choice Paralysis?
> **A:** Too many options (or too much information) causing a person to **freeze** and not decide/act at all.

**Q27.** What is Fusion Investing?
> **A:** Combining **fundamental analysis** (traditional) with **behavioural finance** — integrating the **value-growth** phenomenon and the **momentum** effect.

**Q28.** What did Benjamin Graham say about voting and weighing machines?
> **A:** "In the short run the market is a **voting machine** (sentiment/momentum), and in the long run it is a **weighing machine** (value)."

**Q29.** What are the three steps to identify fusion stocks?
> **A:** Step I: find **value** stocks (P/E, P/BV, P/S); Step II: filter for **fundamental strength** (e.g. Piotroski Score); Step III: pick those with **strong momentum** (price uptrend).

**Q30.** What is the main practical drawback of the momentum step in fusion investing?
> **A:** Momentum is **short-term**, requiring **frequent rebalancing** → higher **transaction costs** and **taxes**, which can erode returns.

**Q31.** What is a market anomaly?
> **A:** A departure of a stock's price from its expected value (per CAPM/asset pricing models) that can't be explained by known or newly released information. Anomalies appear, disappear, and reappear randomly.

**Q32.** How does the disposition effect relate to market anomalies?
> **A:** By holding losers and selling winners, investors create **price momentum** — a persistent anomaly.

**Q33.** Who described bubbles in "Manias, Panics and Crashes," and how is a bubble defined?
> **A:** **Charles Kindleberger (1978)**. A bubble is a sharp, continuous rise in asset price where the initial rise creates expectations of further rises, attracting speculators chasing trading profit.

**Q34.** Why don't rational arbitrageurs immediately pop bubbles?
> **A:** They face a **synchronisation problem** (can't coordinate a selling strategy) and differing incentives to time the market — so bubbles persist longer.

**Q35.** Name four reasons bubbles form.
> **A:** **Liquidity** (cheap money chasing stocks), **Celebrity status** (self-fulfilling media hype), **Momentum** (extrapolation → herd trading), and **Illusion of Control** (believing you can sell before others).

**Q36.** According to Keith Redhead (2008), bubbles are more likely when... (name three conditions).
> **A:** Many **inexperienced traders**; high **uncertainty** about true value; small chance of profit but **very high** potential payoff; purchases financeable by **borrowing**; **short selling is difficult**.

**Q37.** What famous investor lost money in the South Sea Bubble, and what did he say?
> **A:** **Isaac Newton** (~£20,000 loss): "I can calculate the motions of heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people."

**Q38.** How is a crash related to a bubble?
> **A:** A crash is the same process **in reverse** — everyone rushes to sell at once (negative news, liquidity crunch, return to fundamentals), and jostling to exit first triggers the crash.
