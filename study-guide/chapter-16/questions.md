# Chapter 16: Basics of Behavioural Finance — Questionnaire

## 📝 Multiple Choice Questions

**Q1.** Behavioural Finance is best described as the study of:
A) Only stock charts  B) How psychology influences market participants' behaviour  C) How to calculate CAPM  D) Tax planning
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Behavioural finance studies how **psychology** influences market behaviour and explains anomalies standard finance can't.
</details>

**Q2.** Which is NOT an assumption of Standard Finance?
A) Investors are rational  B) Investors have all information  C) Investors are guided by emotional biases  D) Investors are risk averse
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Standard finance assumes rationality; being guided by **emotional biases** is a *behavioural* finance idea.
</details>

**Q3.** Standard Finance is rooted in ___ while Behavioural Finance is rooted in ___.
A) Psychology; Economics  B) Economics; Psychology  C) Law; Finance  D) Statistics; Biology
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Standard finance has **economics** at its core; behavioural finance has **psychology** at its core.
</details>

**Q4.** "Satisficing" means:
A) Maximizing returns  B) Choosing a satisfactory and sufficient (good-enough) option  C) Avoiding all risk  D) Diversifying fully
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Satisficing = satisfactory + sufficient, the hallmark of **bounded rationality**.
</details>

**Q5.** Bounded Rationality, as a concept, means decision-making is:
A) Always irrational  B) Rational under boundary conditions (limited time/info)  C) Random  D) Impossible
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Bounded rationality is **not irrational** — it's rational given limits on time, information, or ability. (Coined by Herbert Simon, 1978.)
</details>

**Q6.** Prospect Theory was introduced by:
A) Benjamin Graham  B) Charles Kindleberger  C) Kahneman & Tversky  D) Herbert Simon
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — **Kahneman and Tversky (1979)**. Herbert Simon is bounded rationality; Graham is value investing; Kindleberger is bubbles.
</details>

**Q7.** According to Prospect Theory, people are:
A) Risk-seeking on gains, risk-averse on losses  B) Risk-averse on gains, risk-seeking on losses  C) Always risk-averse  D) Always risk-seeking
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — People grab **gains early** (risk-averse on gains) but **hold losses longer** hoping to recover (risk-seeking on losses).
</details>

**Q8.** In Prospect Theory, monetary losses compared to equal monetary gains:
A) Feel the same  B) Hurt more  C) Hurt less  D) Are ignored
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A core premise: **losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good.**
</details>

**Q9.** The two broad categories of investing biases are:
A) Big and small  B) Emotional biases and cognitive errors  C) Profit and loss  D) Domestic and foreign
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Emotional biases** (from feelings) and **cognitive errors** (from faulty thinking).
</details>

**Q10.** An investor holds onto losing stocks too long and sells winning stocks too early. This is the:
A) Anchoring bias  B) Framing bias  C) Disposition Effect  D) Choice paralysis
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — The **Disposition Effect** (Shefrin & Statman, 1985), a manifestation of loss aversion.
</details>

**Q11.** Believing that a company with a famous, high-profile CEO must be a good investment is an example of:
A) Endowment bias  B) Stereotyping bias  C) Mental accounting  D) Anchoring
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Stereotyping** — judging by representative characteristics and general perception.
</details>

**Q12.** Confusing a bull market with one's own investing skill is associated with:
A) Status quo bias  B) Overconfidence (and self-attribution) bias  C) Framing  D) Loss aversion
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Overconfidence**, intensified by **self-attribution** ("confusing brains with a bull market").
</details>

**Q13.** An investor refuses to sell inherited shares even though they don't fit her strategy. This is:
A) Endowment bias  B) Anchoring  C) Framing  D) Choice paralysis
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — **Endowment bias** — valuing an asset more simply because you own it (here, also amplified by inheritance and loss aversion).
</details>

**Q14.** An investor never switches a poorly performing fund, fearing the change might turn out badly, so does nothing. This is:
A) Overconfidence  B) Status quo bias (linked to regret aversion)  C) Mental accounting  D) Stereotyping
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Status quo bias**, linked to **regret aversion** — avoiding decisions for fear of regret.
</details>

**Q15.** Spending a year-end bonus extravagantly while being very careful with salary income reflects:
A) Anchoring  B) Framing  C) Mental accounting  D) Overconfidence
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — **Mental accounting** — treating equal money differently based on its mental "bucket" (windfall vs salary).
</details>

**Q16.** "95% lean" beef being preferred over "5% fat" beef (identical product) demonstrates:
A) Anchoring  B) Framing bias  C) Endowment  D) Loss aversion
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Framing** — the answer changes based on how the information is presented.
</details>

**Q17.** Relying heavily on the first price quoted in a negotiation is an example of:
A) Anchoring  B) Mental accounting  C) Status quo  D) Choice paralysis
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — **Anchoring** — over-relying on initial information.
</details>

**Q18.** Benjamin Graham's "voting machine vs weighing machine" idea is central to:
A) Bounded rationality  B) Fusion investing  C) Mental accounting  D) Escheat
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Fusion investing** integrates the short-run "voting machine" (momentum/sentiment) with the long-run "weighing machine" (value).
</details>

**Q19.** Which is the correct order of steps in identifying fusion-investing stocks?
A) Momentum → Value → Fundamentals  B) Value → Fundamentals → Momentum  C) Fundamentals → Momentum → Value  D) Value → Momentum → Fundamentals
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Step I **Value** (P/E, P/BV, P/S) → Step II **Fundamental strength** (e.g. Piotroski Score) → Step III **Momentum** (price uptrend).
</details>

**Q20.** Why do bubbles persist instead of being instantly corrected by arbitrageurs?
A) Arbitrageurs don't exist  B) The synchronisation problem and differing incentives to time the market  C) Governments ban selling  D) Prices never change
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Arbitrageurs face a **synchronisation problem** (can't coordinate selling) and different incentives, so mispricing persists.
</details>

## 🧩 Case Study

**Spotting the biases — Mr. Verma's portfolio**

Mr. Verma is a confident investor. Over the past year he:
1. Bought a single tech stock heavily after a famous investor praised its "star CEO," telling friends "I have a knack for picking winners."
2. Is sitting on a stock down 40% but refuses to sell because "I won't book a loss," while he sold a winner the moment it rose 10%.
3. Keeps three separate "buckets" — a retirement fund (very cautious) and a "fun money" account from a lottery win (which he trades wildly).
4. Was given a list of 60 mutual funds by his adviser and, overwhelmed, has not invested anything for months.
5. Insists the stock is worth ₹500 "because it traded there last year," ignoring the weak current fundamentals.

**Q1.** In point 1, identify the biases at play.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Two: **Stereotyping** (assuming a "star CEO" = a good investment based on general perception) and **Overconfidence** with **self-attribution** ("I have a knack for picking winners" — confusing skill/luck). His heavy single-stock buy also shows portfolio **concentration** typical of overconfidence.
</details>

**Q2.** In point 2, which well-known effect is Mr. Verma displaying, and what underlying bias explains it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The **Disposition Effect** — holding the loser too long and selling the winner too early. It stems from **loss aversion**: the pain of booking a loss outweighs the pleasure of a gain, so he avoids realizing losses but rushes to lock in gains.
</details>

**Q3.** In point 3, what cognitive error is shown, and what would Thaler advise?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Mental accounting** — treating money differently based on its "bucket" (cautious retirement money vs recklessly-traded lottery "fun money"). Thaler would advise treating all money as **fungible** — the same regardless of origin or use — and optimizing the overall portfolio rather than each bucket separately.
</details>

**Q4.** In point 4, which bias is preventing action, and how could an adviser help?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Choice paralysis** — too many options (and too much information) freezing the decision. The adviser could narrow the list to a few suitable options, or use a **nudge** (e.g. a default/auto-enrolment choice) to overcome the inertia.
</details>

**Q5.** In point 5, name the bias and explain why it's risky.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Anchoring** — he anchors the stock's value to its **past price** (₹500) and ignores current weak fundamentals. It's risky because all new information gets filtered through the outdated anchor, leading him to misjudge fair value and potentially hold an overvalued stock.
</details>
