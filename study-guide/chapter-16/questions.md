## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** What does Behavioural Finance primarily study?
A) The mathematics of bond pricing  B) The way psychology influences the behaviour of market participants and its effect on financial markets  C) The legal framework governing stock exchanges  D) The accounting standards for listed companies
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Behavioural Finance is defined as the study of how psychology influences the behaviour of market participants, both individually and as a group, and the subsequent effect on financial markets. It is part of Behavioural Economics. A) bond mathematics, C) legal frameworks, and D) accounting standards are technical/regulatory areas, not the psychology-and-markets focus that defines behavioural finance.
</details>

**Q2.** Behavioural Finance is described as a part of which broader field?
A) Behavioural Economics  B) Corporate law  C) Financial accounting  D) Macroeconomic policy
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — The workbook explicitly states behavioural finance is a part of Behavioural Economics, which deals with biases and cognitive errors affecting investing behaviour. B), C) and D) are unrelated disciplines; behavioural finance specifically grew out of the economics-meets-psychology field of behavioural economics.
</details>

**Q3.** Which of the following is a key assumption of Standard Finance?
A) Investors are emotional and inconsistent  B) Investors rely on thumb rules  C) Investors are rational  D) Investors ignore new information
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Standard finance assumes investors are rational. The other options (emotional/inconsistent, thumb rules, ignoring information) describe real-life behavioural finance observations — the opposite of standard finance's idealised assumptions.
</details>

**Q4.** According to standard finance, investors update their beliefs:
A) Only once a year  B) As new information comes in  C) Never  D) Only when forced by regulators
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A core standard-finance assumption is that rational investors update their beliefs as new information arrives. A) yearly, C) never, and D) only under regulation all contradict the assumption of continuous rational updating.
</details>

**Q5.** "Home bias" — preferring domestic companies because they feel familiar — is an example of:
A) Standard finance working perfectly  B) Real-life behaviour that deviates from standard finance assumptions  C) A regulatory requirement  D) Optimal diversification
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The workbook lists home bias (preferring domestic companies because familiarity makes the risk feel lower) as a real-life behaviour that departs from rational standard-finance theory. A) is wrong because home bias shows standard finance failing; C) it is not a rule; D) it is the opposite of diversification.
</details>

**Q6.** At its core, Standard Finance is based on ______, while Behavioural Finance is based on ______.
A) Psychology; economics  B) Economics; psychology  C) Law; accounting  D) Statistics; geometry
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The comparison table places economics at the core of standard finance and psychology at the core of behavioural finance. A) reverses them; C) and D) name unrelated fields.
</details>

**Q7.** Which two Nobel laureates are credited with bringing Behavioural Finance to the forefront?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Daniel Kahneman (2002) and Richard Thaler (2017) are credited with bringing behavioural finance to the forefront. B) Graham/Buffett are value-investing figures, C) Markowitz/Sharpe are portfolio-theory/CAPM pioneers (standard finance), and D) Fama/Shiller, though Nobel laureates, are not the pair the workbook names here.
</details>

**Q8.** Who is credited with the concept of Bounded Rationality?
A) Daniel Kahneman  B) Richard Thaler  C) Herbert Simon  D) Amos Tversky
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Nobel laureate Herbert Simon (1978) is credited with bounded rationality. A) Kahneman and D) Tversky are linked to prospect theory; B) Thaler is linked to mental accounting and the "nudge."
</details>

**Q9.** "Satisficing" means choosing a solution that is:
A) Optimal and maximising  B) Satisfactory and sufficient  C) Random and careless  D) Always the cheapest
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Satisficing is a blend of "satisfactory" and "sufficient" — a good-enough choice made under limited time/information. A) optimal/maximising is what rationality (not satisficing) seeks; C) random/careless misstates it (satisficing is still rational under constraints); D) cheapest is unrelated.
</details>

**Q10.** Bounded Rationality is best described as:
A) Completely irrational decision making  B) Rational decision making under certain boundary conditions  C) Decision making with perfect information  D) Pure intuition with no analysis
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The workbook stresses bounded rationality is NOT irrational; it is rational decision making under boundary conditions (limited time, information, or ability). A) wrongly calls it irrational; C) describes pure rationality; D) describes pure intuition. Bounded rationality sits between rationality and intuition.
</details>

**Q11.** Under Bounded Rationality, investors process:
A) All available information  B) Only information that is manageable  C) No information at all  D) Only information from regulators
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Step one of bounded rationality is processing only the manageable information, not all of it. A) describes full rationality; C) and D) are not part of the bounded-rationality steps.
</details>

**Q12.** Bounded Rationality sits between which two extremes?
A) Rationality and intuition  B) Greed and fear  C) Profit and loss  D) Risk and return
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Bounded rationality lies between pure rationality (process all info → optimal) and intuition (pure experience). B), C) and D) are real concepts in the chapter but are not the two extremes that bracket bounded rationality.
</details>

**Q13.** Prospect Theory was introduced by:
A) Herbert Simon  B) Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979)  C) Richard Thaler  D) Charles Kindleberger
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Kahneman and Tversky introduced Prospect Theory in 1979. A) Simon is bounded rationality; C) Thaler is mental accounting/nudge; D) Kindleberger wrote on bubbles and crashes.
</details>

**Q14.** According to Prospect Theory, choices are evaluated relative to:
A) The market index  B) A reference point (the person's well-being)  C) The risk-free rate  D) The company's book value
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A premise of prospect theory is that choices are judged relative to a reference point — the investor's own well-being — not in absolute terms. A), C) and D) are objective external benchmarks, not the subjective reference point prospect theory describes.
</details>

**Q15.** According to Prospect Theory, people are:
A) Risk-seeking about gains and risk-averse about losses  B) Risk-averse about gains and risk-seeking about losses  C) Always risk-averse  D) Always risk-seeking
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Prospect theory says people are risk-averse about gains (grabbing them early) but risk-seeking about losses (holding losers longer hoping to recover). A) reverses this; C) and D) ignore that the attitude flips between gains and losses.
</details>

**Q16.** Prospect Theory states that monetary losses:
A) Feel the same as equivalent gains  B) Hurt more than equivalent monetary gains  C) Hurt less than equivalent gains  D) Are always ignored by investors
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A key premise is that losses hurt more than equivalent gains please ("losses are significantly more powerful than gains"). A) says they are equal (false), C) reverses the strength, and D) is wrong because losses are felt very intensely, not ignored.
</details>

**Q17.** Prospect Theory describes two phases of making choices. The first is:
A) The evaluation phase  B) The framing phase  C) The settlement phase  D) The rebalancing phase
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The first phase is the framing phase (prospects are framed using heuristics for a preliminary analysis); the second is evaluation, where the highest-perceived-value prospect is chosen. A) is the second phase; C) and D) are not phases of prospect theory.
</details>

**Q18.** Investing biases broadly fall into which two main categories?
A) Emotional biases and cognitive errors  B) Legal biases and financial biases  C) Short-term and long-term biases  D) Domestic and foreign biases
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Biases are broadly split into emotional biases (from feelings) and cognitive errors (from faulty reasoning). B), C) and D) are not the workbook's categorisation.
</details>

**Q19.** Emotional biases arise from:
A) Faulty statistical reasoning  B) Feelings or emotions, often from deep-rooted personal experiences  C) Memory loss only  D) Reading too many annual reports
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Emotional biases arise spontaneously from feelings/emotions rooted in personal experience and are often protective. A) describes cognitive errors; C) and D) are not the defined source of emotional bias.
</details>

**Q20.** Cognitive errors result from:
A) Reasoning influenced by feelings  B) Faulty thinking — statistical, information-processing or memory errors  C) Following all rules perfectly  D) Having complete information
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Cognitive errors come from faulty thinking — statistical, information-processing, or memory errors. A) describes emotional biases; C) and D) describe ideal rational behaviour, not errors.
</details>

**Q21.** The Disposition Effect refers to:
A) Selling losers fast and holding winners long  B) Holding losers too long and selling winners too early  C) Never selling any stock  D) Buying only new IPOs
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Shefrin and Statman (1985) proposed the disposition effect: investors hold "losers" too long and sell "winners" too early. A) reverses it; C) and D) are unrelated behaviours.
</details>

**Q22.** Believing that a high-profile manager automatically means a better-managed company is an example of:
A) Anchoring  B) Stereotyping bias  C) Mental accounting  D) Choice paralysis
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Judging based on representative characteristics and general perception (e.g., famous manager = good company = good investment) is stereotyping bias. A) anchoring is over-relying on initial numbers; C) mental accounting concerns money buckets; D) choice paralysis is freezing among too many options.
</details>

**Q23.** Overconfidence bias is intensified when combined with which other bias?
A) Endowment bias  B) Self-attribution bias  C) Framing bias  D) Status quo bias
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Overconfidence is intensified when combined with self-attribution bias (people "confuse brains with a bull market," crediting skill for luck). A) endowment, C) framing, and D) status quo are different biases not named as the amplifier here.
</details>

**Q24.** Endowment bias means people value an asset:
A) Less when they own it  B) More when they hold rights to it than when they do not  C) Only at its market price  D) Only at its book value
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Endowment bias is valuing an asset more simply because you own it. A) reverses it; C) and D) describe objective valuation, which is exactly what endowment bias distorts. It combines valuing ownership with loss aversion (e.g., never selling inherited stocks).
</details>

**Q25.** Endowment bias has two attributes. They are:
A) Greed and fear  B) Valuing ownership and loss aversion  C) Anchoring and framing  D) Liquidity and momentum
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The two attributes of endowment bias are valuing ownership and loss aversion. A) greed/fear is a general market description; C) anchoring/framing are cognitive errors; D) liquidity/momentum relate to bubbles.
</details>

**Q26.** Status Quo bias is closely linked to which other bias?
A) Overconfidence bias  B) Regret aversion bias  C) Mental accounting  D) Anchoring
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Status quo bias (taking no decision to avoid regret) is closely linked to regret aversion. A) overconfidence, C) mental accounting, and D) anchoring are unrelated to the inaction-from-fear-of-regret link.
</details>

**Q27.** Regret over actions you took (rather than actions you could have taken) is called:
A) Error of omission  B) Error of commission  C) Error of anchoring  D) Error of framing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Regret over actions taken is an error of commission; regret over actions not taken is an error of omission. A) is the opposite; C) and D) are not types of regret.
</details>

**Q28.** Which type of regret is more intense, leading people to prefer the status quo?
A) Error of omission  B) Error of commission  C) Both are exactly equal  D) Neither causes any regret
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Regret is more intense from errors of commission (acting) than omission (not acting), so people prefer the status quo (inaction). A) understates commission's sting; C) and D) contradict the workbook's point that commission hurts more.
</details>

**Q29.** Mental Accounting bias was developed by:
A) Herbert Simon  B) Richard Thaler (1999)  C) Daniel Kahneman  D) Charles Kindleberger
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The concept of mental accounting was developed by Richard Thaler in 1999. A) Simon is bounded rationality; C) Kahneman is prospect theory; D) Kindleberger is bubbles/crashes.
</details>

**Q30.** "Fungibility" means:
A) Money has labels and cannot be exchanged  B) All money is interchangeable and has no labels  C) Money loses value over time  D) Money must be kept in separate accounts
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Fungibility is the fact that all money is interchangeable and carries no labels. Mental accounting violates this by treating money as less fungible than it is. A) reverses the definition; C) describes inflation; D) describes the mental-accounting error itself.
</details>

**Q31.** Framing bias means a person:
A) Always gives the same answer regardless of wording  B) Answers a question differently based on how it is asked (framed)  C) Refuses to answer any question  D) Anchors to the first price quoted
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Framing bias is answering differently based on how information is presented (e.g., "95% lean" vs "5% fat"). A) is the opposite (rational consistency); C) is refusal, not framing; D) describes anchoring.
</details>

**Q32.** Anchoring bias occurs when people:
A) Diversify across many assets  B) Rely heavily on a pre-existing piece of information when making decisions  C) Sell winners too early  D) Treat money in separate buckets
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Anchoring is over-relying on an initial piece of information (e.g., the first price in a negotiation, or a stock's past price). A) is diversification; C) is the disposition effect; D) is mental accounting.
</details>

**Q33.** Choice paralysis describes a situation where:
A) Too few options force a quick decision  B) Too many options (or too much information) lead to not deciding at all  C) Investors always pick the first option  D) Investors over-trade aggressively
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Choice paralysis is when an overload of options or information leads to inaction (not evaluating or deciding). A) reverses the cause; C) and D) describe other behaviours (default-picking, overconfident trading), not paralysis.
</details>

**Q34.** Mental accounting is classified as which type of bias?
A) Emotional bias  B) Cognitive error (information-processing bias)  C) A standard finance principle  D) A regulatory requirement
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Mental accounting is an information-processing (cognitive) bias. A) is wrong because it stems from faulty processing, not feelings; C) and D) are not biases at all.
</details>

**Q35.** Loss aversion, overconfidence, endowment, stereotyping and status quo are all examples of:
A) Cognitive errors  B) Emotional biases  C) Market anomalies  D) Standard finance assumptions
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — All five are listed in the workbook as emotional biases (from feelings/personal experience). A) cognitive errors are a different category (mental accounting, framing, anchoring, choice paralysis); C) and D) are unrelated concepts.
</details>
