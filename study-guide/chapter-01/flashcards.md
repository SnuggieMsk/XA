# Chapter 1: Basics of Insurance — Flashcards

Cover the answer, recall it, then check.

**Q1.** What are the three basic concepts illustrated by every insurance example?
> **A:** **Risk covered** (the bad event), **Sum assured** (amount paid if it happens), and **Premium** (the small fee paid to join the pool).

**Q2.** In the merchant-ship example, what were the risk covered, sum assured and premium?
> **A:** Risk covered = ship lost at sea; Sum assured = Rs. 10,00,000; Premium = Rs. 20,000. (2 ships lost × Rs. 10,00,000 = Rs. 20,00,000 total loss ÷ 100 merchants = Rs. 20,000 each.)

**Q3.** Why did the King need only Rs. 8,000 per employee to cover a Rs. 20,00,000 sum assured?
> **A:** Only 2 of 500 employees were expected to die. Total loss = Rs. 40,00,000 ÷ 500 = Rs. 8,000 each — just **4%** of the Rs. 2,00,000 salary. Few suffer loss, all share the cost.

**Q4.** What is the difference between risk retention and risk transfer?
> **A:** **Risk retention** = you keep the risk and bear the loss yourself. **Risk transfer** = you pass the risk to an insurer for a premium. Insurance is risk transfer.

**Q5.** What two things does an insurance premium depend on?
> **A:** The **expected loss** and the **probability** of the event occurring during the contract period. Higher probability (e.g., family history of disease) → higher premium.

**Q6.** List the 7 requirements for a risk to be insurable.
> **A:** (a) Large number of exposure units, (b) Insurable interest, (c) Accidental & unintentional, (d) Determinable & measurable, (e) No prospect of gain (pure risk), (f) Chance of loss calculable, (g) Premium economically feasible.

**Q7.** Why is a large number of exposure units important?
> **A:** With many people sharing, only a few suffer loss yearly, so each premium is small. If only 40 of 100 merchants join, premium rises to Rs. 20,00,000 ÷ 40 = **Rs. 50,000** instead of Rs. 20,000.

**Q8.** Death is certain — so how is life insurance possible?
> **A:** Although death is certain, the **timing** of death is uncertain. That uncertainty makes it insurable. It is the one exception to the "loss must be uncertain" rule.

**Q9.** Why is stock-market investing NOT insurable?
> **A:** It is **speculative risk** — it can produce a gain or a loss. Insurance covers only **pure risk** (loss only). Insurance must not create a prospect of profit.

**Q10.** What does the Principle of Indemnity mean?
> **A:** Insurance restores the insured to the financial position they were in **before** the loss — never to a profit. Otherwise people would have no incentive to avoid losses.

**Q11.** What is Utmost Good Faith (Uberrimae Fidei)?
> **A:** Because the insured knows more than the insurer (information asymmetry), the insured must **truthfully disclose all material facts** (family history, smoking, profession). Hiding them lets the insurer reject the claim.

**Q12.** What counts as "material" information, and what's the safe rule?
> **A:** Anything that could change the insurer's perception of risk. **When in doubt, disclose** — a higher premium beats a rejected claim.

**Q13.** What is the test for insurable interest?
> **A:** You're **better off if the risk does NOT happen** and **worse off if it does**. You must face a real monetary loss (not emotional).

**Q14.** Who has insurable interest in a borrower? In an employee?
> **A:** A **lender** has insurable interest in the borrower up to the **amount outstanding**. An **employer** has insurable interest in **key employees**.

**Q15.** What is the difference between Indemnity and Defined Benefit insurance?
> **A:** **Indemnity** pays the **actual measured loss** (health, property, car). **Defined Benefit** pays a **fixed pre-decided sum** without measuring exact loss (life, critical illness, daily hospital cash).

**Q16.** Is life insurance an indemnity or defined-benefit policy? Why?
> **A:** **Defined Benefit** — the exact loss of future income from a death is hard to ascertain, so a fixed sum is paid.

**Q17.** What is subrogation?
> **A:** After paying your claim, the insurer **steps into your shoes** to recover the loss from the at-fault third party. You get paid fast; insurer recoups. Without it, premiums would be higher.

**Q18.** What is contribution and which policies does it apply to?
> **A:** When more than one policy covers the **same risk**, each insurer pays a **proportionate share**. Applies only to **indemnity** policies (loss is measurable; total can't exceed actual loss).

**Q19.** Two flood policies of Rs. 5 lakh each; loss is Rs. 30,000. How is it shared?
> **A:** Equal sum-assured share, so each insurer pays half = **Rs. 15,000**.

**Q20.** In health insurance with multiple policies, when does contribution apply?
> **A:** Per IRDAI, the insured chooses which insurer to claim from. If the claim is **within** that policy's sum insured → no contribution. Only if the claim **exceeds** it do other policies and contribution come in.

**Q21.** What is co-pay? Give the worked example.
> **A:** The **percentage** of a claim you pay. Co-pay 15% on a Rs. 2,00,000 claim → you pay Rs. 30,000, insurer pays Rs. 1,70,000. Higher co-pay → lower premium.

**Q22.** What is a deductible? Give the car example.
> **A:** A **flat amount** you pay first before the claim is payable. Car insurance has a compulsory deductible of **Rs. 1,000** for engines **≤ 1600 CC**. Claim Rs. 25,000 → insurer pays Rs. 24,000.

**Q23.** Policy 1: deductible Rs. 1,000 + co-pay 10%. Policy 2: deductible Rs. 5,000. Claim Rs. 40,000. What does each pay?
> **A:** Policy 1: 40,000 − 1,000 = 39,000; less 10% (3,900) = **Rs. 35,100**. Policy 2: 40,000 − 5,000 = **Rs. 35,000**.

**Q24.** What are the two types of health-insurance deductible?
> **A:** **Compulsory** (fixed by insurer or % of sum assured — used in top-up/super-top-up) and **Voluntary** (you choose; higher deductible → lower premium).

**Q25.** What is cashless claim payment, and what's a caution about it?
> **A:** Insurer pays the hospital/garage directly so you don't block cash. Caution: approved lists keep changing, location may not be your choice, and the process can be slow with deposits demanded.

**Q26.** Why does cashless work more smoothly for corporate (employer) policies?
> **A:** Corporate policies usually cover **pre-existing diseases** from day one (individual policies may not for 3–4 years), and corporates pay large premiums — so fewer disputes.

**Q27.** What is a "Beneficial Nominee" and which section introduced it?
> **A:** Under **Section 39(7)**, Insurance Act 1938 (amended **Feb 2015**), if the life-policy nominee is parent, spouse, children (or any of them), they keep the money **as owners**, not as trustees for legal heirs.

**Q28.** What does Section 45 of the Insurance Act, 1938 say?
> **A:** A life policy **cannot be questioned after 3 years** (from policy/risk commencement or revival, whichever later). During the 3 years it can be repudiated/cancelled only on grounds of **fraud**.

**Q29.** What is the health-insurance moratorium period rule?
> **A:** After **5 continuous years** of unbroken premium payment, no health claim is contestable — **except** for proven fraud and permanent exclusions. If sum insured is enhanced, 5 years restart for the enhanced limit.

**Q30.** Why does "insurance come before investment"? (analogy)
> **A:** Like a batsman wearing pads before batting — protection first. Paying a premium to protect the plan is acceptable before investing for goals.

**Q31.** In a return-of-premium merchant scenario at 12% p.a., why does the premium balloon to Rs. 20,20,000?
> **A:** Rs. 20,000 still goes to the pool; the other Rs. 20,00,000 is invested and grows to Rs. 20,20,000 in a month, returned to the merchant. To get the premium back, total premium must rise above the ship's value — showing the cost of bundling.

**Q32.** What is the ULIP lock-in period, and what two return scenarios must illustrations show?
> **A:** Lock-in = **5 years**. Benefit illustrations must show **4%** and **8%** scenarios, and the prospect must **sign** the illustration.

**Q33.** Within how long can a lapsed ULIP be revived?
> **A:** Within **3 years** from the date of lapsation.

**Q34.** What is the difference between an Insurance Agent and a Direct Broker?
> **A:** An **agent represents the insurer** (gets commission). A **direct broker represents the client** (can charge the client a fee AND earn commission), working with multiple insurers.

**Q35.** How many insurers can a composite Corporate Agent work with?
> **A:** Maximum **3 life + 3 general + 3 health** insurance companies.

**Q36.** What is bancassurance?
> **A:** When a **bank** acts as the corporate agent of an insurance company.

**Q37.** When is an insurance agent/broker exempt from SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013?
> **A:** When they advise **solely on insurance products**. If they also advise on non-insurance securities/investments, IA regulations apply (risk profiling, suitability, fiduciary duty).

**Q38.** From what date are individual health and life insurance/reinsurance policies exempt from GST?
> **A:** From **September 22, 2025**.

**Q39.** What are the three broad insurance need categories?
> **A:** **Income replacement** (life, critical illness, accidental disability), **Income protection** (health, motor), and **Asset protection** (household insurance).

**Q40.** What life events should trigger a review of insurance cover?
> **A:** Marriage, having children, buying a house, a large raise — anything that changes the demands on income. Cover may even be reduced as goals are met.
