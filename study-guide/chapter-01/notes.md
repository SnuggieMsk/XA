## 1.1 Simple Examples That Explain Insurance

Think of insurance as a **group savings pot for bad luck**. Many people put in a little money; the few who actually suffer a loss get paid out of that pot.

### Example 1 — Merchant Ships (19th century London)
- 100 merchants each send 1 ship to India every month. If a ship is lost at sea, the merchant loses **Rs. 10,00,000**.
- On average **2 ships** are lost each month → total monthly loss = `Rs. 10,00,000 × 2 = Rs. 20,00,000`.
- If all 100 merchants each pay **Rs. 20,000** into a fund: `100 × 20,000 = Rs. 20,00,000`.
- That fund exactly pays the 2 unlucky merchants their Rs. 10,00,000 each.

**The concepts born here:**
| Term | Meaning in this example |
|---|---|
| **Risk covered** | Ship lost at sea |
| **Sum assured** (the amount insured) | Rs. 10,00,000 |
| **Premium** (the fee you pay) | Rs. 20,000 |

⚠️ **What can go wrong:**
- (a) Not all 100 merchants join → this breaks the **Law of Large Numbers** (need lots of people for it to work).
- (b) Some merchants want their premium *back* if their ship is safe → this is the idea behind **investment-cum-insurance** policies.

### Example 2 — The King's Employees
- A king has **500 employees**, each earning **Rs. 2 lakh/year for 10 years**. Each is the sole earner for his family.
- Gross lifetime earnings per employee = `Rs. 2,00,000 × 10 = Rs. 20,00,000`.
- **2 employees** are expected to die during the year → income lost = `Rs. 20,00,000 × 2 = Rs. 40,00,000`.
- The king adds **Rs. 8,000 per employee** to a fund: `Rs. 8,000 × 500 = Rs. 40,00,000`.
- That Rs. 8,000 is just **4% of the Rs. 2,00,000 salary**, yet it protects every family.

| Term | Value |
|---|---|
| Risk covered | Loss of income due to death |
| Sum assured | Rs. 20,00,000 |
| Premium | Rs. 8,000 per employee |

💡 **Exam tip:** These two examples are referenced again and again. Memorize the numbers — questions love them.

## 1.2 Need for Insurance

**Insurance is a basic form of risk management** that protects the *economic benefits* you get from **assets**.
- Assets can be **physical** (ships, buildings, machinery) or **human** (a person's earning ability).
- An unexpected event (storm, illness, death, fire) can destroy or reduce the asset's ability to give benefits, and that has a **financial consequence**.

You have two choices when facing a risk:
1. **Risk retention** — you bear the loss yourself.
2. **Risk transfer** — you hand the financial consequence to someone else (an insurer).

> **Insurance = transferring risk** from the **insured** (you) to the **insurer** (the company), which promises to **indemnify** (make good your loss). In return you pay a **premium**.

**What makes an event insurable?** It must cause a **financial loss that can be quantified**. The premium depends on the *expected loss* and the *probability* of the event during the policy period.

💡 **Analogy:** A person whose parents had heart disease, cancer or diabetes pays a *higher* health premium than someone with healthy parents — because the chance the insurer must pay is higher.

### 1.2.1 Requirements of an Insurable Risk
A risk must satisfy ALL of these to be insurable:

| # | Requirement | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| a | **Large number of exposure units** | Many similar units face the same peril, so only a few claim each year (the pool works). |
| b | **Insurable interest** | You must face a real *monetary* loss (not just emotional) if the thing is destroyed. Must be lawful. |
| c | **Accidental & unintentional** | Loss must be by chance, outside your control. *Exception:* death (certain), but its *timing* is uncertain → insurable. |
| d | **Determinable & measurable** | Cause and amount must be provable (e.g., medical bills, death certificate). |
| e | **No prospect of gain/profit** | Must be a **pure risk** (only loss possible). Speculative risk like stock investing is NOT insurable. |
| f | **Chance of loss must be calculable** | Insurer must estimate average frequency & severity. |
| g | **Premium economically feasible** | Premium must be affordable AND far less than the value of the risk, else retaining the risk is smarter. |

> **Principle of Indemnity:** Insurance restores you to where you were *before* the loss — not better. You should not profit from insurance.

💡 **Trap:** Why is the merchant-ship premium Rs. 20,000 if all 100 join, but Rs. 50,000 if only 40 join? Because `Rs. 20,00,000 ÷ 40 = Rs. 50,000`. Fewer people sharing the same total loss = higher premium each. This shows requirement (a) in action.

## 1.3 Fundamental Principles of Insurance

### 1.3.1 Utmost Good Faith (*Uberrimae Fidei*)
- The insured knows more about himself than the insurer — this is **information asymmetry**.
- The insurer uses screening (e.g., **medical tests**) but tests can't reveal everything (family history, habits).
- So the insurer relies on the insured to **disclose all material information truthfully** — family medical history, own medical history, smoking/drinking, profession, etc.
- **Material** = anything that could influence the insurer's view of the risk.

⚠️ **Trap:** If you hide material facts, the insurer can **reject the claim entirely** when the truth comes out at claim time. The low premium you paid is wasted AND the loss isn't covered. **When in doubt, disclose.**

### 1.3.2 Insurable Interest
- The insurer covers a risk only if you have **insurable interest** in the subject matter.
- **Test:** You are *better off* if the risk does NOT happen, and *worse off* if it does.

**Who has insurable interest?**
- A business owner: in his *own* inventory (not a competitor's).
- Family — **direct dependents** and relationships of **blood and marriage**.
- Distant relatives (aunts, uncles, cousins, nieces, nephews): only if there is **proof of financial dependence**.
- A **lender**: in the borrower, up to the amount outstanding.
- An **employer**: in key employees.

## 1.4 Concepts in Insurance

### a) Indemnity Insurance
"A duty to make good any loss, damage or liability." The insurer pays the **actual loss suffered** — so the loss must be **determined first** before payout. *Example:* health insurance reimbursing hospital bills.

### b) Benefit Insurance
A **fixed, pre-estimated sum** is paid when a covered event happens — *without* measuring the exact actual loss. *Example:* life insurance pays a pre-fixed sum on death.

Used when:
- the exact loss is **hard to ascertain** (e.g., loss of future income on death, or in a critical illness), OR
- the loss is **small** and measuring it isn't worth the cost (e.g., a fixed **daily hospital cash allowance**).

> **Table 1.1 — Indemnity vs Benefit (memorize this!):**

| Policy | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Life Insurance | **Defined Benefit** | Exact loss of earnings hard to ascertain |
| Health (hospitalisation reimbursement / Mediclaim) | **Indemnity** | Bills show actual loss |
| Daily Hospital Cash benefit | **Defined Benefit** | Loss small/hard to measure |
| Critical Illness (lump sum) | **Defined Benefit** | Loss of earnings hard to ascertain |
| Property Insurance (fire, flood, earthquake) | **Indemnity** | Loss is ascertainable |
| Car Insurance | **Indemnity** | Loss is ascertainable |

### c) Subrogation
After paying your claim, the insurer **"steps into your shoes"** and pursues the party at fault to recover its money.
- *Example:* Another car hits yours. Your insurer pays you, then chases the at-fault driver/insurer.
- Benefit: you get paid fast; the insurer recovers its loss.
- ⚠️ A policy **without** subrogation rights would be **more expensive** for you.

### d) Contribution
When you have **more than one policy on the same risk**, one insurer can claim a **proportionate share** from the others. Applies **only to indemnity policies** (because total claim can't exceed the actual loss).

**Worked example:** Two flood policies of **Rs. 5 lakh each** (Company A & B). Loss = **Rs. 30,000**. If A pays, it can ask B to chip in `Rs. 30,000 × (5/10) = Rs. 15,000` (equal share of sum assured).

💡 **Health insurance twist (IRDAI rule):** With multiple health policies, *you choose* which insurer to claim from. The chosen insurer **cannot** apply contribution if the claim is **less than** that policy's sum insured. Only if the claim **exceeds** the chosen policy's sum insured do you bring in other policies and apply contribution.

### e) Co-Pay
The **proportion of the claim YOU pay**. Used in car & health insurance.

**Formula:** `Your share = Co-pay % × Claim` ; `Insurer pays = Claim − Your share`

**Worked example:** Co-pay 15%, claim Rs. 2,00,000 → you pay `15% × 2,00,000 = Rs. 30,000`; insurer pays `Rs. 1,70,000`.

**Logic:** Makes you sensitive to costs (you share the bill) and lowers your premium. **Higher co-pay → lower premium.** But it's unpopular because you can't control what hospitals/garages charge.

### f) Deductible
The portion you pay **first**, after which the claim becomes payable. The insurer pays only the **excess over the deductible**.

**Worked example (car):** Deductible Rs. 1,000, claim Rs. 25,000 → insurer pays `25,000 − 1,000 = Rs. 24,000`. (India: compulsory deductible of **Rs. 1,000** for cars up to **1600 CC**.)

**Two types (health):**
1. **Compulsory deductible** — fixed by insurer or as % of sum assured. E.g., deductible Rs. 30,000, bill Rs. 80,000 → insurer pays `Rs. 50,000`, you bear Rs. 30,000. (Top-up/Super Top-up plans use this; their premiums are lower.)
2. **Voluntary deductible** — you choose the amount you'll bear. Higher deductible → lower premium.

### Deductible vs Co-Pay (worked comparison)
- **Policy 1:** deductible Rs. 1,000 + co-pay 10%.
- **Policy 2:** deductible Rs. 5,000, no co-pay.
- Claim = Rs. 40,000.

| Policy | Calculation | Payout |
|---|---|---|
| Policy 1 | `40,000 − 1,000 = 39,000`; then `39,000 − (10% × 39,000 = 3,900)` | **Rs. 35,100** |
| Policy 2 | `40,000 − 5,000` | **Rs. 35,000** |

💡 **Key difference:** A **deductible** is a flat amount subtracted; **co-pay** is a *percentage* you bear (applied after the deductible).

### g) Other Concepts
**Cashless claim payment:** Insurer pays the hospital/garage directly so you don't block your own cash. Relevant for indemnity (health/car). No impact on premium (same claim amount either way).
- ⚠️ Cautions: the cashless network list **keeps changing**; the claim location may not be your favourite hospital; the process can be slow; hospitals may demand a **deposit** that defeats the purpose; pre- and post-hospitalisation still need reimbursement claims.
- **Corporate (employer) policies** run smoother because they usually cover **pre-existing diseases** (individual policies often exclude these for the first 3–4 years), and corporates pay large premiums.

**Nominee vs Legal Heir:**
- Normally a **nominee holds the money in trust** for the legal heirs. (E.g., Mrs. Rich gets the FD but must share with the children if no will.)
- **Beneficial Nominee** (Insurance Act 1938, amended Feb 2015, **Section 39(7)**): if the life-insurance nominee is the **parent, spouse, children, or any of them**, they get the money **beneficially** (keep it as owners, not as trustees).

**Section 45 — 3-year rule:** No life insurance policy can be **called into question after 3 years** from the date of the policy / risk commencement / revival (whichever later). A death claim can't be denied on any ground after 3 years.
- During the first 3 years a claim can be denied/cancelled on grounds of **fraud**.
- Large death claims in the first 3 years invariably trigger investigation. → Always practise utmost good faith.

**Health moratorium — 5 years:** After **5 continuous years** of paid premiums (no breaks), no health claim is **contestable** except for **proven fraud** and **permanent exclusions**. If sum insured is enhanced, the 5 years restart **for the enhanced limit** from the enhancement date.

## 1.5 Role of Insurance in Personal Finance

Insurance protects **income** — the base of every financial plan — from loss, reduction, or a sudden large charge. Examples in the text: death (Siddesh), stroke/critical illness (Manohar), accidental disability (Arushi), car repair (Sindhu), ongoing physiotherapy (Mohit), burglary (Jayant).

> **Insurance comes BEFORE investments.** 🏏 *Cricket analogy:* a batsman always wears protective gear (pads/abdomen guard) before batting — one hit could end his career. Paying premium to protect your plan comes before investing for goals.

## 1.6 Investing Through Insurance

Some policies bundle **risk protection + savings**. The premium splits into an **insurance component** and an **investment component**.

### 1.6.1 Should you invest via insurance?
🏏 *Analogy:* a bat + protective gear can be bought as one kit or separately. Don't buy a combined kit just because it's cheap/convenient if either part is poor quality.

**Merchant-ship "return of premium" math:** If the merchant wants his Rs. 20,000 premium back when the ship returns safe, at **12% p.a. (1%/month)** return:
- He pays **Rs. 20,20,000**. Insurer puts **Rs. 20,000** in the pool, invests **Rs. 20,00,000**.
- After 1 month: `20,00,000 × 1.01 = Rs. 20,20,000` returned to him.
- ⚠️ The premium balloons to **more than the ship's value** — showing how mixing investment + insurance can backfire.

### 1.6.2 Prioritizing insurance vs investment needs
- An insurer's primary job is **risk coverage**, not investments.
- To evaluate an insurance-cum-investment product: **(1) decouple** insurance and investment, **(2) check if risk coverage is adequate** — if not, reject it, **(3)** only then judge the investment return on its own merits.

## 1.7 Role of the Insurance Adviser

### 1.7.1 Steps in Insurance Planning
**a. Identify insurance need** — find situations causing loss/reduction of income or an unexpected charge. Three categories:
- **Income replacement** (life, critical illness, accidental disability, annuities).
- **Income protection** (health, motor — protect income from a large outflow).
- **Asset protection** (household insurance — theft/destruction).

💡 A **young person with no dependents** may need a **personal accident / critical illness** policy more than life insurance. A person **with dependents** needs **life insurance** (income replacement) most.

**b. Estimate the coverage** — match the future value of costs, the protection period, and affordability. **Under-insurance** = you retain part of the risk; **over-insurance** = wasted premium.
- *Tenure example:* Age 30 — a 30-year term plan costs **Rs. 13,800/yr**; a 40-year plan costs **Rs. 16,200/yr**. The extra **Rs. 2,400/yr** buys the option to keep cover past retirement (age 60→70).

**c. Identify the most suitable product.**

**d. Optimise the premium** — share non-mandatory info (motor), choose a higher **deductible**, use **No Claim Bonus**, and take **E-Insurance** (electronic) for discounts.

**e. Monitor the coverage** — review after marriage, children, buying a house, or a big raise. Cover can even be **reduced** as goals are met.

## 1.8 Regulations Pertaining to Insurance

### 1.8.1 Health Insurance (IRDAI)
- **Standard terminology** for terms like pre-existing disease, portability, co-payment, deductible.
- **Standardized exclusions** — insurers can have fewer, never more.
- Mental illness, genetic disorders, congenital disease **removed** from the exclusion list.
- Any health policy (except group / top-up / super top-up) can be **ported** to another company.
- Group policies can port to individual policies (same company in year 1; any company from year 2).
- Insurer must give **written, justifiable reason** for rejecting cover.
- A claim **cannot be rejected** after **5 years** of unbroken policy continuation.
- **Telemedicine** is covered.
- Delay in claim settlement → insurer must pay **interest**.
- All insurers must offer a **standard individual health product** (uniform wording, seamless portability, **no add-ons/optional covers** allowed).
- Individual health policies **exempt from GST** w.e.f. **September 22, 2025**.

### 1.8.2 Unit Linked Insurance Products (ULIP)
- Full disclosure of **charges for each policy year**.
- **Benefit illustrations at 4% and 8%** returns; the prospect must **sign** the illustration.
- **Lock-in period: 5 years.**
- Regular/limited premium ULIPs must have **level (uniform) premiums**.
- A **cap on charges** from the **5th year** onward; charges spread evenly during lock-in (not front-ended).
- Limited-premium ULIPs (except single premium) need a premium-paying term of **at least 5 years**.
- All ULIPs (except pension/annuity) must carry a **mortality or health cover**.
- **Discontinuance charges** capped (as % of fund value & premium, and as an absolute value).
- **No surrender charges after 5 policy years.** Partial withdrawals allowed after the 5-year lock-in.
- A **lapsed ULIP can be revived within 3 years** of lapsation.

### 1.8.3 Insurance Intermediaries
**Intermediary** includes brokers, reinsurance brokers, consultants, surveyors, loss assessors.

| Broker type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Direct Broker** | Charges fee to clients OR earns commission from insurers; arranges insurance with multiple Indian insurers. |
| **Reinsurance Broker** | Arranges **reinsurance** (insurers transferring portions of their risk to other firms to spread risk). |
| **Composite Broker** | Both direct + reinsurance broker. |

- **Insurance Agent:** individual appointed by an insurer to solicit/procure business.
- **Composite Insurance Agent:** agent for 2+ insurers, but **not more than one** life, one general, one health, and one of each mono-line insurer.
- **Corporate Agent:** a company selling an insurer's policies (e.g., a travel agent selling travel insurance).
- **Corporate Agent (composite):** can work with a **maximum of 3 life + 3 general + 3 health** insurers.
- **Bancassurance:** when a **bank** becomes the corporate agent of an insurer.
- **Insurance Web Aggregators:** maintain a website for online price comparison of different insurers' products.

> **Table 1.2 — Agent vs Direct Broker:**

| Parameter | Direct Broker | Insurance Agent | Corporate Agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Represents | **The client** | The insurance company | The insurance company(ies) |
| Works with | Multiple insurers | More than one insurer in each line (life/general/health) if he advises dispassionately | Composite: max 3 life + 3 general + 3 health |
| Can charge | Fees to client **and** commission from insurer | Commission only | Commission only |

### 1.8.4 Intermediary Suitability Rules
- To reduce mis-selling, agents must sell based on **product suitability** (risk appetite, financial goals).
- Suitability had been optional and skipped; now it's required for investment-cum-insurance policies.
- Agents can skip suitability **only with the client's written consent**.
- Benefit illustrations must use **4% and 8%** returns; agents must **sign** them and clearly state **guaranteed vs non-guaranteed** benefits.

### 1.8.5 SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013
- An IRDAI-registered insurance agent/broker giving advice **solely on insurance products** is **exempt** from IA Regulations.
- But if they also advise on **non-insurance securities/investment products**, that advice **is covered** by IA Regulations → they must follow **risk profiling, suitability, and fiduciary responsibility**.

## 📌 One-Page Recap
- **Insurance = risk transfer** from insured to insurer for a **premium**; based on a **pool** where many pay and few claim.
- **Insurable risk** needs: large numbers, insurable interest, accidental loss, measurable loss, pure risk (no gain), calculable chance, feasible premium.
- **Principle of indemnity:** restore, don't profit.
- **Two principles:** **utmost good faith** (disclose everything) and **insurable interest** (you lose money if the risk happens).
- **Indemnity** pays *actual* loss (health, property, car); **Benefit** pays a *fixed* sum (life, critical illness, daily hospital cash).
- **Subrogation:** insurer chases the at-fault party after paying you.
- **Contribution:** multiple indemnity policies share proportionately.
- **Co-pay** = % you bear; **Deductible** = flat amount you bear first. Higher of either → lower premium.
- **Beneficial nominee** (Sec 39(7)): spouse/parent/children keep the money as owners.
- **Sec 45:** no life claim questioned after **3 years** (except fraud earlier). Health **moratorium = 5 years**.
- **Insurance before investments.** Decouple, check cover adequacy, then judge investment.
- **ULIP lock-in = 5 years**, illustrations at 4% & 8%, revive within 3 years.
- **Direct broker = client's side**; **agent = insurer's side**; **bancassurance = bank as corporate agent**.
