## 15.1 The Toolbox

Estate-planning tools split into two boxes — those that work **after death** and those that work **during your lifetime**:

| Used **AFTER death** | Used **DURING lifetime** |
|---|---|
| Will | Family Settlement, Trust, Guardianship, Joint Holding, Gift, Power of Attorney, Mutation |

**Analogy:** Some tools are like a "letter to be opened after I'm gone" (a Will). Others are like "let me set things up now while I'm here" (a gift, a trust).

## 15.2 Wills

> **Will** (Section 2(h), Indian Succession Act, 1925) = the *legal declaration of the intention of the testator about his property, which he wants carried into effect after his death.*

**Key people:**
- **Testator** = the person making the Will.
- **Legatee** = the person named to **receive** a part of the estate.
- **Executor** = the person named to **administer** (carry out) the estate.

A Will **only takes effect after death**. While alive, the testator can **change or revoke** it anytime, and it stays a **confidential** document until executed. (Section 62 confirms revocability while competent.)

**Why write a Will?** No confusion among family; it's personal (express feelings); handles special situations (special needs, minor children, widowed daughter) that the law ignores; can disinherit a disobedient/fraudulent member; offers tax advantages; lets you appoint a faithful **executor** (in intestacy, administrators must post an administration bond).

### 15.2.2 Types of Wills

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Conditional/Contingent** | Takes effect only if a condition happens; if condition fails (or is illegal), the Will is invalid |
| **Joint Will** | Made by 2+ persons; treated as **two separate wills**; takes effect after both die |
| **Mutual Will** | Two testators give each other **reciprocal benefits** (each is both testator and legatee). If testator & legatee are different, a mutual will is **not** viable |
| **Duplicate Will** | A signed & attested **copy** (kept by executor/trustee). If the testator destroys his own copy, **both** are revoked |
| **Holograph Will** | **Entirely in the testator's own handwriting** — considered a strong/good will |
| **Concurrent Will** | Two wills for property in **different countries**; treated independently unless they're related |
| **Sham Will** | Executed with formalities but with **no real testamentary intention** — a nullity |
| **Privileged Will** | For **soldiers in warfare, airmen, mariners at sea** — can be spoken **orally before 2 witnesses**. All others are **unprivileged** wills |

### 15.2.3 Legal requirements & Testamentary Capacity
Section 59 essentials: **testamentary capacity & sound mind, knowledge of contents, free from undue influence/fraud/coercion, voluntary act.**

Who can make a Will? A sound-mind adult (not a minor); a married woman (for property she can alienate); a **deaf/dumb/blind** person if they understand what they're doing; an insane person **during a lucid interval**.

> **Testamentary capacity** = being of sound mind enough to understand your property, your relationship to the people you might leave it to, and the effect of your Will's provisions.

### 15.2.4 Registration of Wills
- Registration is **OPTIONAL** (Section 18, Registration Act, 1908). A **non-registered Will is still genuine**.
- Advantages of registering: can't be tampered/destroyed/stolen; safe custody at registrar; certified copy controlled (Section 57(2)); helps edit leasehold property into heirs' names.
- Registered on **plain paper — no stamp duty**.
- Even after registering, **subsequent wills/amendments need NOT be registered.**
- Exempted from visiting the registrar: a person with bodily infirmity, a person in jail, and a **Muslim pardanashin woman** (registrar can visit them).
- ⚠️ **Special situation:** A bequest to a charitable/religious institution by a **non-Hindu** must be **deposited with the registrar within 6 months** of execution, **and** the testator must **survive 12 months** from execution — else the bequest is invalid.

### 15.2.5 Revoking a Will
A Will is **always revocable** until death (the testator must be of sound mind at revocation). **Four modes of revocation** (Section 62/70):
1. A later **Will or codicil** duly executed
2. A **written declaration** of intention to revoke, executed like a Will
3. **Burning, tearing, or destroying** the Will (by testator or in his presence by his direction) with intent to revoke
4. By a **subsequent marriage**

⚠️ But **Section 57**: for **Hindus, marriage does NOT revoke a Will or codicil**. Also, **divorce** does not revoke a Will. Mere **loss** of a Will is not revocation; merely *intending* to revoke later is not valid.

### 15.2.6 Codicils
> **Codicil** (Section 2(b)) = a **supplementary document** to a Will that explains, alters, or adds to it. It becomes part of the Will and must be **executed and attested just like a Will**.

💡 Use a codicil for **minor** changes (e.g. replacing a deceased trustee). For **major** changes, write a **fresh Will** revoking the old one.

### 15.2.7 Succession Certificate vs Legal Heir Certificate
A **succession certificate** is issued by a court to legal heirs (in **absence of a Will**) to collect the deceased's **debts and securities** (PF, insurance, bank deposits, shares). It does **not** by itself decide ownership/title. (Section 370: if there's a valid Will, the property vests in the executor, so no succession certificate is granted; Section 371: granted by **District Courts**.)

| Feature | **Succession Certificate** | **Legal Heir Certificate** |
|---|---|---|
| Issued by | **Civil Court** | **Tehsildar** of district |
| Purpose | Authority to obtain debts/securities (no Will) | Identifies & establishes living heirs |
| Legal weight | Valid proof under succession laws | NOT conclusive proof |
| Used for | Debts, securities | Insurance claims, pension, PF, gratuity |
| Who can apply | Only the certified legal heir | Spouse, parents, son or daughter |

### 15.2.8 The Executor
> **Executor** (Section 2(c)) = the person to whom the testator entrusts carrying out the Will.

**Duties:** pay funeral/burial expenses & get the death certificate first; locate & value all assets; **pay off all debts, liabilities and taxes** (lenders have first right — may liquidate assets if needed); then distribute the estate; possibly manage a minor's assets until majority. The court grants **probate only to the executor appointed in the Will** (Section 222).

**Who can be executor?** Family/friends/relatives, a beneficiary, or even a **company** (Section 223). A **minor** can be named but probate isn't granted until majority (guardian gets letters of administration). A person of unsound mind **cannot**. There's **no limit** on the number of executors. An executor has a **right of refusal** before starting, but once accepted is legally bound (relieved only by court order).

> **Administrator** = appointed by the **court** when there's no executor, the executor is incapable/refuses, or the executor dies before finishing. Gets **letters of administration**.

## 15.3 Probate

> **Probate** (Section 2(f)) = a copy of a Will **certified under the seal of a court** — i.e. the court's stamp of approval that the Will is **genuine** and the executor's acts are valid.

**Probate vs Letters of Administration:** Probate goes to the **executor**; **letters of administration** are issued when there's **no executor** / he's incapable / he died before proving the Will.

**When is probate mandatory?** If the Will/codicil was made in **Kolkata, Chennai, or Mumbai** (the three Presidency towns), **or** if the immovable property is in those towns. Elsewhere it's **optional**.

⚠️ **Probate is NOT a succession certificate.** Probate is issued **when there IS a Will**; a succession certificate is issued **when there is NO Will**.

**Process:** Executor files an application in the proper court with a copy of the Will → court issues notices to legal heirs + advertises in a national newspaper for objections → petitioner proves the testator's death, valid execution, and that it's the last Will → court grants probate. A **court fee** applies (fixed or % of asset value; varies by state since Wills are a **state subject**).

## 15.4 Gifts, Joint Holding & Nomination

### 15.4.1 Gifts
> **Gift** = a **voluntary transfer** of property **without consideration** (without payment). **Donor** gives; **Donee** receives.

A gift is usually **irrevocable** (revocable only if the donee agrees). Gifts are taxable as "income from other sources" **except**: gifts from **relatives** (spouse, siblings of self/spouse, parents, grandparents, children, grandchildren), gifts on the **occasion of marriage**, and inheritances under a Will. ⚠️ Any **income later earned** from the gift is taxed in the **donee's** hands.

### 15.4.2 Joint Holding
> **Joint Holding** = an asset held by **more than one person**. Modes: **'jointly'** (all must approve), **'either or survivor'**, or **'anyone or survivor'**.

Bank accounts, property, demat, shares, mutual funds can be held jointly. The **first holder** is usually the registered holder. Joint holders can access the asset after the first holder's death. ⚠️ But if heirs contest, **succession laws can override** the joint holder's right.

### 15.4.3 Nomination
> **Nomination** = the right of an investment-holder to appoint who **receives the money** on death.

- Only an **individual** can nominate (not companies, firms, trusts, HUF Kartas, or PoA holders).
- A nominee can be an individual, company, or trust; a **minor** can be a nominee (name a guardian).
- **Multiple nominees** allowed with defined %; **SEBI** raised the limit to **10 nominees**.
- Purpose = **simplify payment** on death, **not** equitable distribution.
- ⚠️ A **Will supersedes a nomination** — the nominee is normally just a **trustee** of the money, not the owner; succession rules generally prevail. **Exceptions:** assets under a separate Act that names heirs — e.g. **Insurance Act (beneficial nominees: spouse/parents/children)** and the **EPF Act** (only immediate family as nominee).
- Proposed banking change: **successive nomination** ("one after another" — alternate nominees).

## 15.5 Family Settlement & Property Transfer

> **Family Settlement** = an instrument to bring **peace** to a family when there's a property dispute/rival claims, avoiding long litigation. Must be among **same-family** members with title/claim/interest, entered **voluntarily and in good faith**.

**Advantages:** Not treated as a **transfer** (so **no capital gains tax**); not a **gift**; **no clubbing** provision. Can be **oral or written**; stamped/registered if required.

### 15.5.2 Forms of property transfer among family

| Form | Tax / cost note |
|---|---|
| **Joint ownership** | No tax on death; capital gains may apply if transferred during lifetime |
| **Gifting** | No tax within blood relatives, but **stamp duty** applies (immovable property must be registered) |
| **Will / Inheritance** | No tax on beneficiary; no re-registration but **mutation** must be applied; capital gains apply only if the inheritor later **sells** (using previous owner's cost) |

### 15.5.3–15.5.6 Family Business
Only about **70%** of family businesses survive a generation. Strategies: **transfer assets early**, use a **limited partnership / ESOP / non-voting stock**, **partial sale** for liquidity, or **create a trust**.

**5 ownership structures:** (1) **Owner-Operator** (one person/couple), (2) **Partnership** (only leaders own — fragile across unequal generations), (3) **Distributed** (all descendants inherit — most sought-after), (4) **Nested** (branches own some assets jointly, some separately), (5) **Public** (some shares publicly traded, run by professional managers).

**Valuation methods:** (1) **Capitalizing of Earnings** (based on cash flow), (2) **Projected Earnings** (= **discounted cash flow**), (3) **Market Approach** (compare similar businesses), (4) **Net Asset Value** (value of assets like real estate/machinery).

**Inter-generational transfer tools:** Succession laws, **Will** (easy but more challengeable/slow), **Gift** (immediate, tax-efficient within blood relatives, but irrevocable + stamp duty), and **Trust** (cost-effective, sustainable, long-term).

### 15.5.7 Joint Tenancy vs Tenancy-in-Common

| Feature | **Joint Tenancy** | **Tenancy-in-Common** |
|---|---|---|
| Right of survivorship? | **Yes** — on death, share passes to surviving tenant | **No** — share passes by Will/succession |
| Can leave share by Will? | **No** (unless sole survivor) | **Yes** |
| How acquired | Same time, same transaction, identical interest | Can be unequal shares (else deemed equal) |

### 15.5.8 Asset Protection
> **Asset Protection** = strategies/laws to shield assets from creditors' claims.

Factors deciding how much protection is needed: **identity of debtor**, **identity of creditor** (government is more powerful), **nature of claim**, **nature of asset** (e.g. **PPF** can't be attached by creditors).

**Strategies:** (1) Use **Corporations/LLPs** (owners not personally liable, except fraud), (2) **Asset Protection Trusts (APTs)** — assets not legally owned by you so creditors can't reach them (drawback: **irrevocable**, you lose control), (3) **Transferring property rights** to spouse/relative/friend (risky; fraudulent transfers can still be unwound).

### 15.5.9 Creditor Protection Period
Timeline of laws: RDDBFI Act 1993 (first tribunal) → SARFAESI Act 2002 (secured creditors can seize assets) → CICRA (credit bureaus like CIBIL) → **Insolvency & Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016** (a turnaround, with **defined timelines**). Under IBC: file application → adjudicating authority ascertains default within **14 days** → admit/reject → communicate admission within **7 days** → corporate insolvency resolution process.

## 15.6 Trusts

> **Trust** (Section 3, Indian Trust Act, 1882) = an obligation attached to ownership of property, arising from a **confidence** reposed in and accepted by the owner, for the benefit of another (or another + the owner).

**Analogy:** You hand your money to a trusted friend (trustee) with strict instructions to use it for your child (beneficiary). The friend holds it but must follow your rules.

**Parties / terms:**
- **Author / Settlor** = the person who creates the trust and sets aside the property.
- **Trustee** = the person who **accepts** and **manages** the property (holds legal title).
- **Beneficiary** = the person who **benefits**.
- **Trust property** = the subject matter; **instrument of trust** = the document (trust deed).

**Why form trusts?** Charitable/religious goals; Income Tax exemptions (Sec 10/11); welfare of dependent family; managing PF/superannuation/gratuity funds.

**Essential elements (5):** an **author/settlor**, a **trustee**, a **beneficiary**, the **trust property**, and a **specific object**. (Section 6: author must show intention, purpose, beneficiary, trust property, and transfer the property to the trustee.)

**Advantages:** welfare of dependents; reduces litigation; manages charitable distributions; preserves wealth; gifts to minors/elderly care; **strong tax-planning tool**; **avoids probate** (passes directly to beneficiaries).
**Disadvantages:** complex; expensive to set up/maintain; high stamp duty for immovable property; **settlor loses control**; trustees' powers limited by the deed.

### 15.6.2 Classification of Trusts

| Trust type | Key idea |
|---|---|
| **Public Trust** | For the general public/a section (charitable/religious — temples, schools, hospitals) |
| **Private Trust** | For specific individuals/families |
| **Revocable Trust** | Settlor keeps control, can modify/terminate; **NOT** asset-protective; taxed in settlor's hands; acts as an **alternative to a Will** |
| **Irrevocable Trust** | Cannot be amended/revoked unless the deed expressly allows (Sec 77/78) |
| **Simple Trust** | Trustee has **no active duties**; beneficiary can demand possession |
| **Specific Trust** | For a special purpose; trustee must actively act |
| **Oral / Written** | Oral allowed **only for movable** property; **immovable** needs a written deed |
| **Implied / Express** | Implied = inferred from circumstances; Express = formal trust deed |

### 15.6.3 Characteristics: Discretionary vs Determinate

| Feature | **Discretionary Trust** | **Determinate (Specific) Trust** |
|---|---|---|
| Trustee discretion | **Full** discretion over income/corpus | **No** discretion — fixed shares per deed |
| Beneficiary shares | **Not** defined/determinate | **Clearly specified** (or computable) |
| Taxation | **Maximum Marginal Rate (MMR) = 30%** | Taxed on the representative assessee (Sec 161(1)) at slab rates |

💡 **Exceptions** where a discretionary trust is NOT taxed at MMR: (a) no beneficiary has other trust income/income above threshold, or (b) it's the **sole trust created under a Will** to support **dependent relatives**.

### 15.6.4 Types of Family Trust
Public Trust (Charitable / Religious — permanent, indefinite) vs Private Trust (defined beneficiaries). Private trusts are of two types: **Private Discretionary** and **Private Specific**. A **Public-cum-Private Trust** splits income between public and private purposes (the private portion is taxable).

### 15.6.5 Family Trust vs Will (very important distinctions)

| Feature | **Will** | **Family Trust** |
|---|---|---|
| When effective | **Only on death** | **As soon as created** |
| Distribution timing | At death | Before/at/after death |
| What it covers | Property in your name at death | Property **transferred to** the trust |
| Probate | **May** go through probate | **No** probate |
| Privacy | Can become **public record** | Stays **private** |
| Special uses | Name a **guardian** for minors | Plan for **disability / tax saving** |

### 15.6.6 Parties to Trust (recap)
**Settlor** (creates, can amend if living trust), **Trustee** (manages, has legal title), **Beneficiary** (gets benefits — primary & residual). In a private trust, beneficiary and author can be the **same person**.

### 15.6.7 Hybrid Trust
Has features of **both** determinate and discretionary: trustee pays a **fixed amount** as set by the settlor, and has **discretion** over the rest.

### 15.6.8 Cancellation / Revocation of a Private Trust
The Act doesn't provide for "dissolving"; a trust becomes **extinct** when its purpose is fulfilled/unlawful/impossible, or if revoked. A trust can be revoked: with **consent of competent beneficiaries**; if created to **pay the settlor's debts**; or if a **non-testamentary/verbal** trust reserved revocation power for the settlor.

### 15.6.9 Tax Efficiency
- **Public (charitable) trust** — exemptions under Sections 11, 12, 12A, 12AA, 12AB, 13.
- **Private trust** — governed by Sections 160, 161, 162, 164; income taxed at **slab rate** or **MMR** depending on structure.
- **Revocable trust** — income taxed in **transferor's (settlor's) hands** (Sec 60–62). Sec 62 exception: not so if the trust is irrevocable during the beneficiary's lifetime and the transferor gets no benefit.
- **Business income** of a trust → taxed at **MMR** (exception: sole Will-created trust for a dependent relative → slab rate).
- **Clubbing of minor income** (Sec 64) — a minor's income clubs with the parent's, *except* a minor with a **Section 80U disability** (not clubbed).
- **Discretionary trust** → **MMR** (with exceptions).

### 15.6.11 Trust Perpetuities
A trust **cannot be perpetual**. Income can't be accumulated longer than the **transferor's lifetime or 18 years** from transfer (whichever is later). For a Will, accumulation beyond **18 years** from the testator's death is void. **Exception:** trusts for public benefit (religion, knowledge, commerce, health, safety).

### 15.6.12 Trust as a Pass-Through Entity
> **Pass-through (flow-through) entity** = income "flows through" to the beneficiaries; it's treated as **their** income, not the entity's.

A private trust has **no separate legal identity**. The receipts by the trustee on behalf of the beneficiary are **not taxable** — what's taxable is the **beneficiary's income** (trustee pays in a **representative** capacity).

### 15.6.13 Stamp Duty & Capital Gains on Transfer
- **Stamp duty** (Article 58, Central Stamp Act) is payable on the **trust deed** when registered (a **state** matter). No stamp duty when distributing to beneficiaries. ⚠️ Settling **immovable property during the settlor's lifetime** → stamp duty applies; settling **through a Will** → **no** stamp duty.
- **Capital gains:** Sec **47(iii)** exempts transfers to an **irrevocable trust** from capital gains. Transfers to a **revocable trust** **are** taxed. A **testamentary** transfer (on death, via Will) is exempt.

### 15.6.14 Offshore Trusts
Trusts formed **outside India** under foreign laws. India places **no restriction** on non-resident trustees/beneficiaries. Taxation: **revocable** offshore trust → income taxed as **settlor's**. **Irrevocable** offshore trust: (1) all beneficiaries Indian residents → trustee taxed in representative capacity; (2) mixed residents → only the **Indian** beneficiary taxed on what they receive; (3) partial control in India → taxed as **Association of Persons**.

### 15.6.15 Distributable Net Income (DNI)
> **DNI** = the portion of a trust's income **allotted to the beneficiaries** (becomes their income, taxed in their hands). Its purpose is to **avoid double taxation**.

**Formula:**
> **DNI = Taxable Income − Capital Gains + Tax Exemptions**
> *(If there's a capital **loss**, add it instead of subtracting the gain.)*

And **Taxable Income** = interest + dividends + capital gains − fees − exemptions.

💡 **Worked example (from the chapter):** Trust ABC: interest ₹50,000 + dividends ₹50,000 + capital gains ₹20,000 − fees ₹20,000 − exemptions ₹20,000 = **Taxable Income ₹80,000**.
Then **DNI** = ₹80,000 − ₹20,000 (capital gains) + ₹20,000 (exemption) = **₹80,000**.

## 15.7 Guardianship

> **Guardianship** = the legal process of deciding who steps in to care for a person who can't care for themselves (a minor, an incapacitated adult, or an elderly parent).

A guardian's role:
- **Guardian of the Person** → custody and care.
- **Guardian of the Estate** → managing **financial affairs**.

**Three kinds:**
1. **Natural Guardian** — falls **by birth** (parents are natural guardians until the child turns **18**).
2. **Court-appointed Guardian** — for those with no parents/incapacitated; court appoints.
3. **Testamentary Guardian** — appointed under a **Will** (mostly for minor children).

The applicant must be an **Indian citizen over 18**. A guardian makes financial decisions (bank accounts, investments, availing loans/concessions for the disabled) for an incapacitated adult.

## 15.8 Power of Attorney (POA)

> **POA** = a legal document giving one person (the **agent / attorney-in-fact / donee**) the power to act for another (the **principal / donor**).

Both parties must be **major, of sound mind, and competent to contract**. Used heavily for property transfer and by **NRIs** / when a principal is ill/absent.

⚠️ Two exceptions on capacity: a **married woman can execute a POA even if a minor**; and even a **minor can be a donee** (Sec 184), but a **lunatic cannot** be an attorney.

### 15.8.1 Types of POA
1. **General POA** — broad authority, multiple transactions.
2. **Special POA** — narrow, only a specific transaction; authority **ends when the transaction completes**.
3. **Special POA for Registration** — authorises presenting a document for registration.

### 15.8.2 Revocation of POA
A principal can cancel a POA anytime. Revoked (Sec 201) when: principal revokes; principal/agent becomes **insolvent**; holder **renounces**; business is **completed**; or on **death** of either party. ⚠️ Sec 202: if the **agent has an interest** in the agency, it **can't** be revoked without the agent's consent.

**Authentication (Sec 85, Evidence Act):** by a **Notary Public, court/judge/magistrate, Indian Consul/Vice-Consul, or a Central Government representative.**

**Registration:** A POA is **NOT compulsorily registrable** (it doesn't create/transfer interest in property) — **unless** it creates an interest/charge in immovable property. **Stamp duty** (Article 48) applies and varies by state.

### 15.8.3 Limits of a POA holder
The agent: can't act outside the document's terms; can't change the document or transfer control to a non-designated person; **loses all control once the principal dies**.

### 15.8.4 POA Executed Abroad
NRIs can execute a POA abroad for Indian matters (banking, property). It must be **authenticated/attested by the Indian Embassy/Consulate** and notarized where executed. It remains valid even after the person returns to India.

## 📌 One-Page Recap
- Tools: **after death** = Will, Nomination; **during life** = Trust, Gift, Family Settlement, Joint Holding, Guardianship, PoA, Mutation.
- **Will**: effective only on death, always revocable; **testator** makes it, **legatee** receives, **executor** carries out. Registration is **optional**.
- **Codicil** = minor amendment to a Will. **Probate** = court certifying a genuine Will (mandatory in Kolkata/Chennai/Mumbai).
- **Succession certificate** (no Will, civil court, debts/securities) ≠ **Legal heir certificate** (Tehsildar, identifies heirs) ≠ **Probate** (with Will).
- **Gift** = voluntary, no consideration; tax-free among relatives but income from it is taxed in the donee's hands.
- **Nomination** simplifies payment; a **Will supersedes a nomination** (except insurance/EPF beneficial nominees).
- **Trust** = settlor → trustee → beneficiary. **Revocable** = settlor keeps control, taxed on settlor; **Irrevocable** = no control, protects assets, capital-gains exempt (Sec 47(iii)).
- **Discretionary trust** = trustee discretion, taxed at **MMR (30%)**; **Determinate** = fixed shares, slab rate.
- **Trust vs Will**: trust = effective now, private, no probate; Will = on death, can be public, may need probate.
- **DNI = Taxable Income − Capital Gains + Exemptions.**
- **POA**: General (broad) / Special (one transaction); not compulsorily registrable; dies with the principal.
