**Q1.** What does the term "life expectancy" refer to?
A) The age at which one must retire  B) The number of years an individual is expected to live  C) The number of working years  D) The number of years pension is paid
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Life expectancy is the number of years an individual is expected to live, depending on factors like health and scientific advancement.
- A) Wrong: that is the retirement age, a separate concept.
- C) Wrong: working years run from start of career to retirement age, not until death.
- D) Wrong: pension period is "years during retirement," a different figure.
</details>

**Q2.** Why has the need for retirement planning increased over time?
A) Falling life expectancy  B) Increasing life expectancy  C) Rising salaries  D) Falling inflation
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — As average life expectancy rises, people spend more years in retirement with no salary, so they must plan to fund those years.
- A) Wrong: life expectancy is rising, not falling.
- C) and D) Wrong: rising salaries and falling inflation would ease, not create, the need; the driver named in the workbook is longer life.
</details>

## 🧩 Case Study

**Scenario:** Mr. Verma is **40 years old** and plans to retire at **60** (so **20 years to retirement**). He expects to live to **80** (**20 years during retirement**). His current monthly household expense is **Rs. 50,000**. He assumes **general inflation of 6% p.a.** His retirement investments are expected to earn a **nominal return of 8% p.a.** Separately, his **health costs** of **Rs. 8,000/month** today are expected to rise at **10% p.a.** (faster than general inflation, as the workbook notes). Use the workbook's Expense Protection approach and formulas.

Helpful multipliers: 1.06^20 ≈ 3.207; 1.10^20 ≈ 6.7275; 1.06^1 = 1.06.

**Q-CS1.** What will Mr. Verma's general monthly household expense (excluding health) be at the start of retirement?
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Formula: Future Cost = Present Cost × (1 + i)^n.** Here Present = Rs. 50,000, i = 6% = 0.06, n = 20.
Future Cost = 50,000 × 1.06^20 ≈ 50,000 × 3.207 = **Rs. 1,60,350 per month**.

**Intuition:** At 6% inflation, costs roughly triple over 20 years (multiplier ≈ 3.21), so today's Rs. 50,000 lifestyle costs about Rs. 1.60 lakh a month when he retires.
</details>

**Q-CS2.** What will his monthly HEALTH cost be at retirement, and why is it projected separately?
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Formula: Future Cost = Present Cost × (1 + i)^n**, using the *health* inflation rate of 10%.
Future health cost = 8,000 × 1.10^20 ≈ 8,000 × 6.7275 = **Rs. 53,820 per month**.

**Why separate:** The workbook states health costs (and travel) typically rise at a *higher* rate than standard inflation. Using 6% would understate them badly (8,000 × 3.207 ≈ Rs. 25,656). At 10% the figure is more than double that, so projecting health separately avoids under-funding.
</details>

**Q-CS3.** What is the real (inflation-adjusted) rate of return on his retirement corpus during distribution?
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Formula: Real Return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1.** Nominal = 8% = 0.08, inflation = 6% = 0.06.
Real Return = (1.08 ÷ 1.06) − 1 = 1.018868 − 1 = 0.018868 ≈ **1.89% p.a.**

**Intuition:** Although the money earns 8%, prices rise 6%, so the real growth in purchasing power is only about 1.89%, not the rough 2% you'd get by simple subtraction. This small real return is what actually helps the corpus last through retirement.
</details>

**Q-CS4.** Combining CS1 and CS2, what is his TOTAL monthly expense at the start of retirement?
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Total = future household expense + future health cost** (each compounded at its own rate).
Total = Rs. 1,60,350 (household at 6%) + Rs. 53,820 (health at 10%) = **Rs. 2,14,170 per month**.

**Intuition:** The Expense Protection Method sums the projected expense heads. Because health inflates faster, it grows from a small share today (8,000 of 58,000 ≈ 14%) to a much bigger share at retirement (53,820 of 2,14,170 ≈ 25%). This is exactly why the workbook warns to budget extra for health and travel.
</details>

**Q-CS5.** Verma is tempted to delay saving by 10 years (cutting "years to retirement" from 20 to 10). Using workbook logic, what happens to the monthly saving he needs, and what risk does he add?
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Effect on savings:** The workbook states "longer the period, greater the compounding effects, and lower the allocation from savings required." Cutting the horizon from 20 to 10 years gives compounding far less time, so the **monthly saving needed rises sharply** to reach the same corpus.

**Added risk:** Delaying also makes the corpus harder to build (the workbook calls late starts "catastrophic" because required amounts grow manifold) and leaves less room to recover from poor markets. Combined with **longevity risk** (he could live beyond 80) and rising health costs, delaying significantly raises the chance of an **under-funded retirement**.

**Intuition:** Time is the cheapest ingredient in retirement planning. Each year of delay must be paid for with much larger contributions later, because you lose years of compound growth that money cannot easily buy back.
</details>
