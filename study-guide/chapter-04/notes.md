## 4.1 Why Retirement Planning Is Needed

**Simple idea:** When you stop working, the salary stops — but the bills don't. Retirement planning is about building a pot of money (a **corpus**) that pays your bills for the rest of your life.

**Jargon defined:**
- **Life expectancy** = the number of years a person is expected to live. It keeps rising because of better healthcare and science.
- **Corpus** = the total lump sum of money you save up for retirement.

**Analogy:** Imagine filling a water tank during the monsoon (your working years) so you have water all through the dry summer (retirement). If the summer turns out longer than expected, you'd better have filled the tank higher.

**Worked example from the book:** X is in his 20s. Retirement age = 60, so working life = 40 years. If life expectancy = 80, then X must fund **20 years** of life with NO salary. That's 20 years of expenses to pay from savings.

**The biggest roadblock:** accumulating *enough* money. Early in your career, responsibilities (house, marriage, kids) push retirement to the "back burner" — but those same responsibilities are exactly *why* you must plan.

**Three phases of retirement planning:**
1. **Preparation stage** — child education, buying a house, getting life & health insurance, recognising the impact of ageing.
2. **Pre-retirement (initial retirement) stage** — physical/psychological changes; you learn the retirement rules and procedures.
3. **Final retirement** — arrangements complete; you decide how to live.

💡 **Tip:** Start early. The earlier you start, the more time compounding has to do the heavy lifting for you.

---

## 4.2 Why Retirement Is Different From Other Goals

People plan many goals at once: kids' education, a car, a house, retirement. But retirement is **special**:

- **No loans for retirement.** You can borrow for a house, car, or education. **No bank gives a loan to fund your retirement.** So you *must* save for it yourself.
- **It gets postponed.** Education has a fixed deadline (a child starts college in ~14–15 years; you can't delay 4–5 years). Retirement, by contrast, is easy to push back "just a few years" — and people raid their retirement savings for other goals.

⚠️ **Trap:** Delaying retirement planning can be **catastrophic** — the required amount grows manifold, and you lose the early-start (compounding) benefit.

## 4.3 The Retirement Planning Process

The retirement goal is unique: it has the **longest accumulation and distribution periods** and needs the **largest corpus**.

**Two key stages (defined):**
- **Accumulation stage** = the years you save and invest to *build* the corpus.
- **Distribution stage** = the retirement years when the corpus is *used* to generate income.

**The steps:**

**a) Determine expenses in retirement.** Expenses won't be the same as before. Heads include: housing (utilities, maintenance, taxes), living expenses (food, personal upkeep), medical care, transportation, recreation, insurance (life, health, disability), and taxes.

**b) Determine income requirement.** Consider: maintaining the standard of living, the expenses to be met, and the inflation rate.

**c) Time horizon.** Two periods matter:
- **Years to retirement** = current age → retirement age. Shrinks every year. The longer this is, the bigger the inflation effect on future cost, *but* the greater the compounding benefit (so lower savings needed per month).
- **Years in/during retirement** = start of retirement → end of life. Can't be known exactly; estimated from average life expectancy, gender, health, genetics, lifestyle.

⚠️ **Longevity risk:** underestimating how long you'll live means the money runs out before you do.

**d) Determine the retirement corpus.** Variables: (i) periodic income required, (ii) expected inflation, (iii) rate of return the corpus earns, (iv) period of retirement.

---

### 4.3.1 Impact of Inflation

**Definition:** **Inflation** is a general rise in prices over time. As prices rise, one rupee buys less — inflation "eats away the purchasing power of money."

**Formula — Future Cost (compounding inflation):**

> **Future Cost = Present Cost × (1 + inflation rate)^number of years**

- **Present Cost** = what it costs today
- **inflation rate** = yearly rate prices rise (as a decimal, e.g. 6% = 0.06)
- **number of years** = how far in the future

**Logic:** Prices rise by a *percentage each year on the new (already-risen) price* — that's compounding, just like interest. So costs snowball.

**Worked example from the book (6% inflation, item costs Rs. 100 today):**
| Years ahead | Cost |
|---|---|
| 10 | Rs. 100 × 1.06^10 = **Rs. 179** |
| 20 | Rs. 100 × 1.06^20 = **Rs. 321** |
| 30 | Rs. 100 × 1.06^30 = **Rs. 574** |

**Inflation hits retirement TWICE:**
1. It raises the *cost of expenses* by the time you retire.
2. It keeps raising expenses *during* retirement (your monthly need rises every year).

**Book example (living on Rs. 60,000/month today, 6% inflation):**
- In 10 years you'd need **Rs. 1,07,451/month**
- In 20 years you'd need **Rs. 1,92,428/month**
…just to maintain the same lifestyle.

💡 **Tip:** Health costs and travel usually rise *faster* than general inflation — budget extra for them.

### 4.3.2 The Expected Rate of Return & Real Rate of Return

The corpus is **invested** both while accumulating and while distributing, so it keeps earning a return. That return reduces how much you must save.

- **Higher expected return → lower corpus needed.** BUT higher return = higher risk.
- In **accumulation**, you can afford more risk (long horizon). In **distribution**, your ability to take risk falls (you can't afford big losses).

**Inflation and return pull in opposite directions:**
- Inflation **pushes UP** the corpus required.
- Return on the corpus **pushes DOWN** the savings required.

**Definition:** **Real (inflation-adjusted) rate of return** = the return on an investment *after* adjusting for inflation.

**Formula — Real Rate of Return:**

> **Real Return = [(1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation rate)] − 1**

- **nominal return** = the headline return your investment earns
- **inflation rate** = the rate prices rise

**Logic:** Money grows by the nominal return, but the "finish line" (prices) also moves up by inflation. The real return is how much *purchasing power* you actually gained.

**Worked example (return 8%, inflation 6%):**
Real Return = (1.08 ÷ 1.06) − 1 = 1.01887 − 1 = **0.01887 ≈ 1.89% p.a.**
(Note: simply subtracting gives ~2%, but the precise formula gives 1.89%.)

## 4.4 Estimating Retirement Corpus — Two Methods

### 4.4.1 Replacement Ratio Method

**Idea:** Assume your standard of living stays the same as just before retirement. You "replace" a percentage of your pre-retirement income.

**Definition:** **Replacement ratio** = the fraction of your last working income you want to keep receiving in retirement (e.g. 80% or 50%).

**Formulas:**
> **Replacement Income (Year 1) = Pre-retirement Income × replacement ratio**
> **Each subsequent year = previous year's income × (1 + inflation)** — to keep purchasing power

**Book example A (quick):** Income Rs. 2,00,000, 80% ratio → plan for Rs. 1,60,000 in year 1.

**Book example B (Mr. Rajeev):**
- Age 50, earns Rs. 1,00,000/month now, retires at 60, wants 50% replacement.
- Income grows 10% p.a. → income at retirement = Rs. 1,00,000 × 1.1^10 = **Rs. 2,59,374 p.a.**
- Year-1 replacement income = 2,59,374 × 0.50 = **Rs. 1,29,687**
- Year-2 (inflation 7%) = 1,29,687 × 107/100 = **Rs. 1,38,765**, and so on.

**Reductions in living expenses & taxes:** At retirement, some expenses fall (housing loan, kids' education, work-related costs) and some long-term savings stop. Income often drops you into a **lower tax bracket**, cutting tax. All these *reduce* the income needed.

⚠️ **Limitation:** The more years to retirement, the *less accurate* the replacement estimate. But starting early still lowers the return you need to hit your target.

### 4.4.2 Expense Protection Method

**Idea:** Build the retirement income from a **detailed monthly budget** of actual expenses. Some go up (medical, travel, gifting, house maintenance); some go down (housing loan, kids' education, income tax). Easier for people *near* retirement.

**Book example (Mr. Ashish):**
| Particulars | Amount (Rs.) | Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| Current household expense | 30,000 | 50% of 60,000 |
| Additional expenses at retirement | 15,000 | — |
| Total retirement expense | 45,000 | 30,000 + 15,000 |
| Years to retire | 20 | 60 − 40 |
| Inflation rate | 6% | — |
| **Expenses at retirement** | **1,44,321** | 45,000 × 1.06^20 |

⚠️ **Limitation:** Like the replacement method, it's hard to estimate future expenses far in advance — review periodically.

## 4.5 Superannuation Benefits to Employees

**Definition:** **Superannuation** is an organisational pension program a company creates for its employees (also called a **company pension plan**). Funds grow, typically tax-free, until retirement/withdrawal.

**Key law point:** In India there is **no statutory superannuation** except for employees under the **Employees' Pension Scheme, 1995**. Government bodies, nationalised banks and a few others *may* pay pension if they decide. All other establishments (public or private) must arrange **annuities through LIC or an IRDAI-approved life insurer**.

**Two arrangements an employer can use:**
1. **Payment by the employer**
2. **Funding through a Trust**

### 4.5.1 Payment by the Employer
- (a) Pay a lump sum out of current revenue to buy an **immediate annuity** from a life insurer (reward for a few employees), OR
- (b) Pay pension from current revenue as it falls due — **not allowed/not satisfactory**.

⚠️ **Why (b) fails:** Pension payment depends on the employer's profits; if profits fall or the employer goes bankrupt, pensioners may not get paid. The liability also keeps growing.

- **Accounting Standard 15 (ICAI):** retirement benefits must be provided for in the *year of accrual*, not when they fall due.
- **Section 2(11), Insurance Act:** makes it obligatory to arrange superannuation only through an IRDAI-registered life insurer. So the employer must **create a fund** and set aside money on an accrual basis each year.

### 4.5.2 Funding Through a Superannuation Trust
- Employer creates an **irrevocable Trust**, distinct from the employer, and appoints **Trustees**.
- Employer transfers contributions (employer's + employees', if contributory) to the Trust Fund.

### 4.5.3 Approved Superannuation Funds
The employer wants the contributions to be **tax-deductible**, so the fund must be **approved by the Commissioner of Income Tax**.

**Conditions for approval (Part B, Fourth Schedule, IT Act):**
- Established under an **irrevocable trust**; not less than **90% of employees employed in India**.
- Sole purpose = provide **annuities** on retirement at/after a specified age, or on incapacity, or to widows/children/dependents on death.
- The **employer must be a contributor**.
- All annuities/pensions/benefits **payable only in India**.

**Tax benefits of an approved superannuation fund:**
| Section | Benefit |
|---|---|
| **10(13)** | Payments from the fund on death, on commutation of annuity at/after a specified age, on incapacity, or on transfer to NPS (80CCD) are **not included in total income** (exempt). |
| **10(25)(iii)** | Income earned by trustees on behalf of the fund is **not taxed** → the fund grows tax-free. |
| **36(iv)** | Employer's contribution is allowed as a **business expense deduction** (within prescribed limits/CBDT conditions). |
| **80C** | Employee's contribution is **deductible** (within the IT Act ceiling). |

### 4.5.4 Trustees' Responsibilities
- Hold periodic meetings; keep minutes.
- Keep in touch with the company; inform employer of decisions.
- Collect contributions (or get them calculated by an **Actuary** in a defined benefit scheme).
- Invest Trust money per the IT Rules pattern; reinvest interest.
- Arrange pension benefits per options exercised.
- **Deduct TDS** on annuity payments (or have the insurer do so).
- Prepare, audit and submit annual accounts to IT authorities.

## 📌 One-Page Recap
- Retirement is unique: **longest horizon, biggest corpus, no loans available** → you must self-fund.
- Process: estimate expenses → income needed → time horizon → corpus.
- **Inflation** raises costs (compounding): Future Cost = Present × (1+i)^n. It hits both *at* and *during* retirement.
- **Return** lowers savings needed; **higher return = higher risk**; take less risk in distribution.
- **Real return = (1+return)/(1+inflation) − 1.** Example: 8% & 6% → **1.89%**.
- Two estimation methods: **Replacement Ratio** (% of last income) and **Expense Protection** (budget-based).
- **Superannuation** = company pension; in India must go through LIC/IRDAI insurer or a Trust; tax benefits under 10(13), 10(25)(iii), 36(iv), 80C; needs Commissioner of Income Tax approval.
