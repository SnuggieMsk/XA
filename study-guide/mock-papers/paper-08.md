## Section A — Numerical MCQs (40 questions × 1 mark = 40 marks)

**Q1.** A client invests ₹6,00,000 in a portfolio that is 60% equity (returns 14% p.a.) and 40% debt (returns 7% p.a.). What is the weighted (blended) expected return of the portfolio for one year?
A) 10.50%  B) 9.80%  C) 12.60%  D) 11.20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Blended return = (weight₁ × return₁) + (weight₂ × return₂). Plug in: (0.60 × 14%) + (0.40 × 7%) = 8.4% + 2.8% = 11.2%. A) 10.5% is the simple average of 14 and 7 (ignores the 60/40 weights). C) 12.6% wrongly uses 90/10 weights. B) 9.8% mistakenly weights debt at 60%. The lesson: portfolio return is the *weighted* average of each asset's return, weighted by how much money sits in each.
</details>

**Q2.** Inflation is 6% and a bank FD gives a nominal return of 7.2%. What is the real (inflation-adjusted) return?
A) 1.20%  B) 1.13%  C) 0.72%  D) 13.20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1. Plug in: (1.072 ÷ 1.06) − 1 = 1.01132 − 1 = 0.01132 = 1.13%. A) 1.20% is the rough shortcut (7.2 − 6.0), which slightly overstates the true real return. C) 0.72% just halves wrongly. D) adds them. The exact Fisher formula divides instead of subtracting, so the answer is a touch below 1.2%.
</details>

**Q3.** An investor sells listed equity shares held for 18 months and books a long-term capital gain of ₹3,25,000 in FY 2025-26. What is the LTCG tax under Section 112A (ignore surcharge/cess)?
A) ₹40,625  B) ₹24,375  C) ₹25,000  D) ₹65,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Under Section 112A, listed-equity LTCG is taxed at 12.5% only on the amount above the ₹1,25,000 annual exemption. Taxable gain = ₹3,25,000 − ₹1,25,000 = ₹2,00,000. Tax = 12.5% × ₹2,00,000 = ₹25,000. A) ₹40,625 forgets the exemption (12.5% × ₹3,25,000). B) ₹24,375 uses a wrong base. D) ₹65,000 applies 20% (the STCG rate). Holding > 12 months = long-term for listed equity, so 112A applies.
</details>

**Q4.** An investor sells listed equity shares held for 7 months with a short-term gain of ₹1,80,000 (FY 2025-26, STT paid). What is the STCG tax under Section 111A (ignore surcharge/cess)?
A) ₹22,500  B) ₹6,875  C) ₹36,000  D) ₹18,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — STCG on listed equity under Section 111A is taxed at a flat 20% with NO basic exemption like the ₹1.25 lakh. Tax = 20% × ₹1,80,000 = ₹36,000. A) ₹22,500 wrongly uses 12.5%. B) ₹6,875 wrongly subtracts the ₹1.25 lakh first then applies 12.5% — the exemption is only for LTCG. D) ₹18,000 uses an old 10% rate. Holding ≤ 12 months for listed equity = short-term.
</details>

**Q5.** A 30-year-old client wants ₹40 lakh (today's value) for a goal 10 years away. Inflation is 6% p.a. What is the inflation-adjusted future cost?
A) ₹64,00,000  B) ₹70,00,000  C) ₹71,63,391  D) ₹66,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Future cost = Present cost × (1 + inflation)^n = ₹40,00,000 × (1.06)^10. (1.06)^10 = 1.790847, so cost = ₹40,00,000 × 1.790847 = ₹71,63,391. A) ₹64,00,000 wrongly uses simple inflation (40 + 6%×10 ≈ 60%). B) ₹70,00,000 and D) ₹66,00,000 under-compound. Money loses value as prices compound, so you must grow the goal at compound inflation.
</details>

**Q6.** A portfolio's value grew from ₹5,00,000 to ₹9,00,000 over 4 years. What is the CAGR?
A) 20.00%  B) 12.59%  C) 15.83%  D) 16.86%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — CAGR = (End ÷ Start)^(1/n) − 1 = (9,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000)^(1/4) − 1 = (1.8)^0.25 − 1. (1.8)^0.25 = 1.15829, so CAGR = 15.83%. A) 20% is the simple average annual growth (80% total ÷ 4 years). B) 12.59% and D) 16.86% mis-root. CAGR is the steady compounded rate that turns the start value into the end value over n years.
</details>

**Q7.** A client's monthly take-home is ₹1,20,000 and monthly expenses are ₹85,000. What is the monthly surplus available for investing (savings rate to nearest %)?
A) ₹35,000 and 70%  B) ₹35,000 and 41%  C) ₹40,000 and 33%  D) ₹35,000 and 29%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Surplus = income − expenses = ₹1,20,000 − ₹85,000 = ₹35,000. Savings rate = surplus ÷ income = 35,000 ÷ 1,20,000 = 29.2% ≈ 29%. B) 41% wrongly divides by expenses (35,000 ÷ 85,000). C) miscomputes the surplus. A) 70% is the expense ratio, not the savings rate. Savings rate is always surplus as a fraction of income.
</details>

**Q8.** Using the "100 minus age" rule of thumb for equity allocation, a 42-year-old has a corpus of ₹50 lakh. What is the suggested equity rupee allocation?
A) ₹21,00,000  B) ₹25,00,000  C) ₹42,00,000  D) ₹29,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — The rule says equity % = 100 − age = 100 − 42 = 58%. Equity allocation = 58% × ₹50,00,000 = ₹29,00,000. A) ₹21,00,000 is the *debt* portion (42% × 50 lakh). C) puts age% directly into rupees wrongly. B) is a 50/50 split. The rule lowers equity as you age because you have less time to recover from market falls.
</details>

**Q9.** A tax-saving bank FD pays 7% p.a. The investor is in the 30% slab. What is the post-tax return on the FD?
A) 7.00%  B) 4.90%  C) 2.10%  D) 5.39%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — FD interest is fully taxable each year at the slab rate. Post-tax return = nominal × (1 − tax rate) = 7% × (1 − 0.30) = 7% × 0.70 = 4.90%. A) 7% ignores tax. C) 2.10% is the tax *taken away*, not what's left. D) 5.39% wrongly applies a lower rate. FD interest gives no concessional treatment, so a high earner keeps only 70% of the headline rate.
</details>

**Q10.** PPF pays 7.1% p.a., fully tax-free (EEE). For an investor in the 30% slab, what taxable FD rate would be needed to match PPF's post-tax return?
A) 7.10%  B) 9.23%  C) 10.14%  D) 8.52%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — A tax-free 7.1% must be grossed up: equivalent taxable rate = tax-free rate ÷ (1 − tax rate) = 7.1% ÷ (1 − 0.30) = 7.1% ÷ 0.70 = 10.14%. A) 7.1% ignores PPF's tax-free edge. B) 9.23% uses 0.769 (a 23% slab) instead of 0.70. D) 8.52% multiplies by 1.20. Because PPF interest is fully exempt, a taxable product must yield ~10.1% pre-tax to leave the same in hand.
</details>

**Q11.** A 60/40 equity-debt portfolio of ₹10,00,000 drifts to ₹7,80,000 equity and ₹4,20,000 debt after a rally. How much equity must be sold to rebalance back to 60/40?
A) ₹60,000  B) ₹1,08,000  C) ₹48,000  D) ₹30,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — New total = ₹7,80,000 + ₹4,20,000 = ₹12,00,000. Target equity = 60% × ₹12,00,000 = ₹7,20,000. Current equity ₹7,80,000 − target ₹7,20,000 = ₹60,000 to sell. B) ₹1,08,000 rebalances to the wrong total. C) and D) use the old ₹10 lakh base. Rebalancing trims the asset that grew (sell high) and tops up the laggard (buy low), restoring the policy mix.
</details>

**Q12.** A SIP of ₹10,000 per month runs for 5 years (60 months) earning 12% p.a. (1% per month). Using the future value of annuity formula FV = P × [((1+i)^n − 1) ÷ i], what is the approximate maturity value?
A) ₹6,00,000  B) ₹7,80,000  C) ₹8,16,697  D) ₹6,72,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — FV = 10,000 × [((1.01)^60 − 1) ÷ 0.01]. (1.01)^60 = 1.81670, so [(1.81670 − 1) ÷ 0.01] = 81.670. FV = 10,000 × 81.670 = ₹8,16,697. A) ₹6,00,000 is only the money invested (no growth). B) ₹7,80,000 and D) ₹6,72,000 under-compound. SIP value grows because each instalment compounds for the months remaining.
</details>

**Q13.** Required return: a client needs ₹1 crore in 8 years and can invest ₹50 lakh today as a lump sum. What annual return must the corpus earn?
A) 12.50%  B) 9.05%  C) 8.00%  D) 7.18%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Required return = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (1,00,00,000 ÷ 50,00,000)^(1/8) − 1 = (2)^0.125 − 1. (2)^0.125 = 1.09051, so 9.05%. A) 12.5% is the simple growth (100% ÷ 8). C) and D) are too low. To double money in 8 years you need just over 9% compounded (matches the "Rule of 72": 72 ÷ 8 = 9%).
</details>

**Q14.** Under EPF, an employee earns a basic salary of ₹40,000/month. The employer's 12% contribution is split into EPS (8.33% of wages, capped at ₹15,000 wage ceiling) and EPF. What is the monthly EPS contribution?

D) ₹4,800 is the total 12% on ₹40,000. B) ₹1,800 mixes up the ceiling. EPS is always limited to the ₹15,000 wage ceiling, so the rest of the employer's 12% flows into EPF.
</details>

**Q18.** A debt mutual fund (purchased after 1 April 2023) is sold after 3 years with a gain of ₹2,00,000. How is it taxed for an investor in the 30% slab?

**Q22.** An investor compares two funds: Fund A returned 13% with standard deviation 18%; Fund B returned 10% with standard deviation 10%. Risk-free rate is 6%. Which has the higher Sharpe ratio?
A) Fund A (0.39)  B) Both equal  C) Fund B (0.40)  D) Fund A (0.72)
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Sharpe = (return − risk-free) ÷ standard deviation. Fund A = (13 − 6) ÷ 18 = 7 ÷ 18 = 0.389. Fund B = (10 − 6) ÷ 10 = 4 ÷ 10 = 0.40. Fund B is marginally higher (0.40 > 0.39). A) miscalls the winner. B) ignores the difference. D) forgets to subtract the risk-free rate. Sharpe rewards return *per unit of risk*, so a lower-return, much-lower-risk fund can win.
</details>
