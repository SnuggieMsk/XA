# Mock Paper 1 — NISM Series X-B (Investment Adviser Level 2)
**Total: 100 marks · 70 questions · Suggested time: 2 hours · Negative marking: 25% of the marks for a wrong answer.**

> This paper is numerical and case-heavy — almost every question needs a calculation. Keep a spreadsheet or calculator (and the Cost Inflation Index table) handy, and read each scenario carefully before you compute. All transfers are assumed to be on or after 23 July 2024 unless stated otherwise.

## Section A — Numerical MCQs (40 questions × 1 mark = 40 marks)

**Q1.** Ravi sells listed equity shares (STT paid on buy and sell) held for 3 years. His total long-term capital gain for the year is ₹4,00,000. How much LTCG tax does he pay under Section 112A (ignore surcharge/cess)?
A) ₹50,000  B) ₹15,625  C) ₹40,000  D) ₹34,375
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Section 112A taxes LTCG on listed equity (STT paid) at **12.5%**, but the **first ₹1,25,000 of gain each year is exempt**. Formula: Tax = (Total LTCG − ₹1,25,000) × 12.5%. Plug in: (₹4,00,000 − ₹1,25,000) = ₹2,75,000 taxable; ₹2,75,000 × 12.5% = **₹34,375**. A) ₹50,000 wrongly taxes the full gain at 12.5% (forgets the exemption). C) ₹40,000 uses 10% on the full gain (the old pre-2024 rate). B) ₹15,625 taxes only ₹1,25,000 by mistake. The ₹1.25 lakh shelter is a per-year freebie, like a tax-free slice of the cake before the knife (12.5%) cuts the rest.
</details>

**Q2.** Priya has a short-term capital gain of ₹3,00,000 on listed equity shares (STT paid), covered by Section 111A. What is her STCG tax (ignore surcharge/cess)?
A) ₹45,000  B) ₹37,500  C) ₹60,000  D) ₹30,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — STCG covered by **Section 111A** (listed equity/equity MF/business trust units, STT paid, held ≤12 months) is taxed at a flat **20%** after the Finance Act 2024. Tax = ₹3,00,000 × 20% = **₹60,000**. A) ₹45,000 uses 15% (the old rate before 23 July 2024). B) ₹37,500 uses 12.5% (that's the LTCG rate, not STCG). D) ₹30,000 uses 10%. There is no basic exemption for STCG under 111A — the whole gain is taxed.
</details>

**Q3.** A residential house was bought in FY 2009-10 for ₹30,00,000 and sold in FY 2024-25 (before 23 July 2024) for ₹1,20,00,000. CII: 2009-10 = 148, 2024-25 = 363. What is the indexed cost of acquisition?
A) ₹89,99,999  B) ₹30,00,000  C) ₹66,00,000  D) ₹73,58,108
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Indexed cost = Cost × (CII of year of sale ÷ CII of year of purchase). This "inflates" the old cost so you are not taxed on inflation. Plug in: ₹30,00,000 × (363 ÷ 148) = ₹30,00,000 × 2.4527 = **₹73,58,108** (approx). B) ₹30,00,000 ignores indexation. C) ₹66,00,000 just doubles arbitrarily. A) uses wrong index ratio. CII raises the cost from ₹30L to about ₹73.58L, shrinking the taxable gain.
</details>

**Q4.** Using Q3's figures, the property is sold **before** 23 July 2024 (old provisions). What is the LTCG tax @20% on the indexed gain (ignore surcharge/cess)?
A) ₹9,28,378  B) ₹18,00,000  C) ₹5,80,236  D) ₹7,42,702
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Indexed gain = Sale − Indexed cost = ₹1,20,00,000 − ₹73,58,108 = ₹46,41,892. Old provisions tax it @20%: ₹46,41,892 × 20% = **₹9,28,378**. B) ₹18,00,000 taxes the non-indexed gain (₹90,00,000) at 20%. C) ₹5,80,236 mistakenly uses 12.5% on the indexed gain. D) ₹7,42,702 applies 12.5% on ₹59.4L. For property bought before 23 July 2024 and sold before that date, the old indexation-plus-20% rule applies.
</details>

**Q5.** An investor buys 200 listed equity shares at ₹500 each (1 June 2023) and sells all on 1 March 2024 at ₹620 each. Brokerage ₹400 total. STT is paid. Holding period and tax head?
A) Short-term, taxed under 111A at 20%  B) Short-term, taxed at slab rate  C) Long-term, taxed under 112A  D) Long-term, taxed under 112 at 12.5%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Listed equity shares are **long-term only if held more than 12 months**. Here the holding is 1 June 2023 to 1 March 2024 = about 9 months, so it is **short-term**. STT is paid, so it falls under **Section 111A and is taxed at 20%**. C) and D) wrongly assume long-term. B) is the rate for non-111A STCG (not this case, since STT is paid on a listed share). The 12-month line is the key test for listed equity.
</details>

**Q6.** Continuing Q5: compute the short-term capital gain (net of brokerage).
A) ₹24,000  B) ₹120 per share  C) ₹24,400  D) ₹23,600
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — STCG = Sale value − Cost − Transfer expenses. Sale = 200 × ₹620 = ₹1,24,000; Cost = 200 × ₹500 = ₹1,00,000; brokerage (transfer expense) = ₹400. STCG = ₹1,24,000 − ₹1,00,000 − ₹400 = **₹23,600**. A) ₹24,000 forgets to deduct brokerage. C) ₹24,400 wrongly adds brokerage. Note: STT is **not** deductible, but brokerage is. B) is just the per-share gain, not the total.
</details>

**Q7.** An equity share was bought on 01-01-2017 at ₹100; its FMV on 31-01-2018 was ₹200; it is sold on 01-01-2025 at ₹250 (STT paid). What is the LTCG per share under grandfathering?
A) ₹150  B) ₹0  C) ₹50  D) ₹250
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Grandfathering: cost = higher of (actual cost) or (lower of FMV on 31-01-2018 and sale price). Actual cost = ₹100. Lower of (₹200, ₹250) = ₹200. Higher of (₹100, ₹200) = ₹200. LTCG = ₹250 − ₹200 = **₹50**. A) ₹150 uses the original ₹100 cost (ignores grandfathering). B) ₹0 wrongly caps at FMV. D) ignores cost entirely. Grandfathering protects gains that accrued up to 31-01-2018 from tax.
</details>

**Q8.** Same share as Q7 but sold at ₹150 (FMV on 31-01-2018 was ₹200, cost ₹100). LTCG per share?
A) ₹50  B) ₹0  C) −₹50 (loss)  D) ₹150
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Cost = higher of (actual ₹100) or (lower of FMV ₹200 and sale ₹150 = ₹150) = ₹150. LTCG = ₹150 − ₹150 = **₹0**. The rule prevents grandfathering from turning a real gain into an artificial loss. A) ₹50 uses ₹100 cost. C) wrongly creates a loss using ₹200. D) ignores cost. When sale price sits between actual cost and FMV, the gain is nil.
</details>

**Q9.** Mr A sold a long-term capital asset (other than a house) for net consideration ₹50,00,000 with LTCG ₹20,00,000, and invested ₹40,00,000 in one residential house. What is the Section 54F exemption?
A) ₹20,00,000  B) ₹8,00,000  C) ₹10,00,000  D) ₹16,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Section 54F exempts proportionately: Exemption = LTCG × (Amount invested ÷ Net consideration). Plug in: ₹20,00,000 × (₹40,00,000 ÷ ₹50,00,000) = ₹20,00,000 × 0.8 = **₹16,00,000**. A) ₹20,00,000 wrongly assumes full exemption (that needs the entire net consideration to be invested). B) and C) use wrong fractions. 54F looks at net **consideration** (not just the gain) when working out the proportion.
</details>

**Q10.** Under Section 54EC, the maximum exemption available by investing LTCG from land/building in NHAI or REC bonds within 6 months is:
A) ₹1,00,00,000  B) ₹50,00,000  C) ₹20,00,000  D) No limit
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Section 54EC caps the exemption at the lower of (the amount invested in specified NHAI/REC bonds) or **₹50,00,000**. So even if you invest more, only ₹50 lakh of gain is sheltered. A) ₹1 crore is wrong. C) ₹20 lakh is the gratuity ceiling, not 54EC. D) "no limit" is wrong — there is a hard ₹50 lakh cap. Investment must be made within 6 months of transfer.
</details>

**Q11.** An unlisted bond bought in 2020 is redeemed on maturity in October 2024 at a gain of ₹2,00,000. How is this gain taxed?
A) Short-term capital gain at slab rate  B) LTCG at 20% with indexation  C) LTCG at 12.5%  D) Exempt
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — After the Finance Act 2024, an unlisted bond/debenture transferred or maturing after 23 July 2024 is **always short-term** under Section 50AA, taxed at slab rates — regardless of how long it was held. B) and C) treat it as long-term (wrong). D) "exempt" is wrong. So even a 4-year holding gives short-term treatment for unlisted bonds.
</details>

**Q12.** Units of a Specified Mutual Fund (debt fund investing 65%+ in debt) acquired in 2023 and sold in 2025 at a gain are taxed as:

**Correct: B)** — Gains from Specified Mutual Funds (schemes with 65%+ in debt/money-market) acquired on/after 1 April 2023 are **always short-term**, taxed at slab rates, irrespective of holding period (Section 50AA). </details>

**Q15.** Mr B has LTCG of ₹90,000 from listed equity (STT paid) for the year. How much tax under 112A?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — LTCG up to ₹1,25,000 from listed equity is **fully exempt**. Since ₹90,000 < ₹1,25,000, tax = **₹0**. C) ₹11,250 wrongly applies 12.5% to the whole gain. B) ₹9,000 uses 10%. D) doubles it. The first ₹1.25 lakh each year is shielded entirely.
</details>

**Q16.** Bonus shares allotted on 01-04-2020 are sold in FY 2024-25. What is their cost of acquisition for capital gains?
A) Face value  B) Market price on allotment  C) FMV on 31-01-2018  D) Nil
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Bonus shares issued on/after 01-04-2001 have a cost of acquisition of **Nil** (you paid nothing for them). So the entire sale value (subject to grandfathering if pre-31-01-2018) becomes gain. A), B) and C) are wrong because no money was paid to obtain bonus shares. The holding period, though, runs from the date of allotment of the bonus shares.

**Q17.** Short-term capital loss can be set off against:
A) Only short-term capital gains  B) Any head of income  C) Both short-term and long-term capital gains  D) Only long-term capital gains
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — A **short-term capital loss** can be set off against **both** short-term and long-term capital gains. (A long-term capital loss, by contrast, can only be set off against long-term gains.) A) is too narrow. B) is wrong — capital losses cannot offset salary/business income. D) is the rule for long-term losses, not short-term. STCL is the more flexible of the two losses.
</details>

**Q18.** Long-term capital loss can be set off against:
A) Only long-term capital gains  B) Both short-term and long-term gains  C) Any income  D) Salary income
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — A **long-term capital loss can only be set off against long-term capital gains** (and carried forward for 8 years). B) describes a short-term loss. C) and D) are wrong — capital losses never touch salary or other heads. LTCL is "fussy": it pairs only with LTCG.
</details>

**Q19.** Mr C earns ₹1,00,000 LTCG from listed equity (STT paid) and ₹2,00,000 LTCG from sale of gold (held 3 years). Total LTCG tax (ignore surcharge/cess)?

Gold LTCG of ₹2,00,000 is taxed at 12.5% (no ₹1.25 lakh shelter — that is only for 112A equity): ₹2,00,000 × 12.5% = **₹25,000**. A) ₹37,500 wrongly taxes ₹3,00,000 at 12.5%. C) ₹34,375 applies the exemption to gold too. B) is a miscalculation. The ₹1.25 lakh exemption applies only to listed-equity LTCG.

**Q20.** No deduction under Sections 80C to 80U is allowed against which income?

**Q21.** Securities Transaction Tax (STT) paid on sale of shares is:
A) Deductible from sale consideration  B) Deductible as cost of acquisition  C) Not deductible while computing capital gains  D) Added to the gain

**Q22.** Mr D (covered by Payment of Gratuity Act) retires after 24 years. Last drawn salary (basic + DA) = ₹52,000/month; gratuity received = ₹8,00,000. Exempt amount?
A) ₹8,00,000  B) ₹7,20,000  C) ₹20,00,000  D) ₹6,24,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Exemption = least of: (i) ₹20,00,000; (ii) Last salary × 15/26 × completed years = ₹52,000 × 15 ÷ 26 × 24 = ₹52,000 × 0.576923 × 24 = **₹7,20,000**; (iii) actual ₹8,00,000. Least = **₹7,20,000**. A) is the actual received (not least). C) is the statutory cap (not least). D) uses 1/2 month instead of 15/26. The taxable portion is ₹8,00,000 − ₹7,20,000 = ₹80,000.
</details>

**Q23.** Under EPF, the employer contributes 12% of (basic + DA). How is the employer's 12% split between EPF and EPS (EPS capped at ₹15,000 salary)?

**Correct: A)** — Of the employer's 12%, **3.67% goes to EPF** and **8.33% goes to EPS** (Employees' Pension Scheme), with EPS limited to a monthly salary ceiling of ₹15,000. B) reverses the split. C) and D) are wrong. The employee's own 12% goes entirely to EPF.

**Q30.** Health policy has a compulsory deductible of ₹30,000. Hospital bill is ₹80,000. How much does the insurer pay?

**Q31.** A health policy has a co-pay of 15%. The ascertained claim is ₹2,00,000. How much does the insurer pay?

**Q32.** Policy 1 has a deductible of ₹1,000 AND a co-pay of 10%. Claim = ₹40,000. Insurer pays?

**Q34.** Real rate of return when nominal return is 10% and inflation is 6%?
A) 4.00%  B) 4.24%  C) 3.77%  D) 16.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1 = (1.10 ÷ 1.06) − 1 = 1.03774 − 1 = **3.77%**. A) 4% is the rough subtraction (10% − 6%), which overstates. B) is a miscalculation. D) just adds. The exact formula divides rather than subtracts, giving slightly under 4%.
</details>

**Q35.** An investment grows from ₹1,00,000 to ₹2,00,000 in 6 years. The CAGR is approximately:
A) 16.67%  B) 12.25%  C) 11.49%  D) 10.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — CAGR = (End ÷ Start)^(1/n) − 1 = (2,00,000 ÷ 1,00,000)^(1/6) − 1 = 2^(0.1667) − 1 = 1.1225 − 1 = 0.1149 = **11.49%**. A) 16.67% is simple-interest thinking (100% ÷ 6). B) 12.25% is the square-root-style error. D) is a guess. Doubling in 6 years implies about 11.5% compounded (consistent with the "Rule of 72": 72 ÷ 6 = 12, close).
</details>

**Q36.** Today's annual expense is ₹6,00,000. With 6% inflation, what will the same expense cost in 10 years (round to nearest ₹)?
A) ₹9,60,000  B) ₹6,36,000  C) ₹10,00,000  D) ₹10,74,509
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Future cost = Present × (1 + inflation)^n = ₹6,00,000 × (1.06)^10 = ₹6,00,000 × 1.79085 = **₹10,74,509**. A) ₹9,60,000 uses simple interest (6% × 10 × 6L = 3.6L added). C) is a round guess. B) inflates for just one year. Inflation compounds, so the cost nearly doubles in a decade at 6%.
</details>

**Q37.** A retiree's pre-retirement annual expense is ₹12,00,000. Using a replacement ratio of 70%, the estimated annual retirement expense is:

A) Insurer ₹1,00,000; Farhan ₹0  B) Insurer ₹60,000; Farhan ₹40,000  C) Insurer ₹40,000; Farhan ₹60,000  D) Insurer ₹85,000; Farhan ₹15,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Deductible is borne by the insured first; insurer pays the rest. Insurer = ₹1,00,000 − ₹40,000 = **₹60,000**; Farhan bears the ₹40,000 deductible. A) ignores the deductible. C) reverses it. D) treats it like a 15% co-pay. The deductible is a fixed first slice the insured pays before cover kicks in.
