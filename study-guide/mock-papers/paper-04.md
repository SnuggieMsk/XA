# Mock Paper 4 — NISM Series X-B (Investment Adviser Level 2)
**Total: 100 marks · 70 questions · Suggested time: 2 hours · Negative marking: 25% of the marks for a wrong answer.**

> This paper is numerical and case-heavy, with a strong focus on the taxation of debt and "other" products (Sec 50AA, SGB, gold, REIT/InvIT/AIF, ULIPs) and on time-value-of-money maths (FV, PV, CAGR, real returns). ## Section A — Numerical MCQs (40 questions × 1 mark = 40 marks)

**Q1.** Mr. A buys an unlisted bond of an Indian company for ₹4,80,000 and sells it 5 years later for ₹8,00,000. Under the post-2023 rules, what is the nature of his capital gain?
A) Long-term, taxed at 12.5%  B) Long-term, taxed at 20%  C) Exempt  D) Short-term, taxed at slab rates
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — For unlisted bonds/debentures, gains are ALWAYS treated as short-term capital gains irrespective of the holding period (Table 10.1), and STCG on bonds is taxed at the assessee's applicable slab rates. So the ₹3,20,000 gain (8,00,000 − 4,80,000) is short-term at slab rates. A is the rule for *listed* bonds held >12 months. B and C invent rates/exemptions that don't exist here. The trap is assuming a 5-year hold automatically means long-term — for unlisted bonds the holding period is simply ignored.
</details>

**Q2.** Mr. P holds a listed bond bought for ₹1,20,000 and sells it after 20 months for ₹1,70,000. What is his capital-gains tax (ignore cess/surcharge)?
A) ₹10,000  B) ₹6,250  C) ₹6,000  D) ₹5,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Listed bonds qualify as long-term if held >12 months; 20 months > 12 months, so it is LTCG. LTCG on listed bonds = 12.5% without indexation. Gain = 1,70,000 − 1,20,000 = ₹50,000. Tax = 50,000 × 12.5% = ₹6,250. A wrongly uses 20%; C wrongly uses 12% (50,000 × 12%); D wrongly uses 10%. Formula: Tax = (Sale − Cost) × 12.5%.
</details>

**Q3.** A Specified Mutual Fund (Type 1, investing 80% in debt) was bought on 10-May-2023 for ₹2,00,000 and redeemed on 20-June-2028 for ₹3,10,000. How is the ₹1,10,000 gain taxed?
A) LTCG at 12.5% with indexation  B) LTCG at 12.5% without indexation  C) STCG at slab rates  D) Exempt as held > 36 months
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Section 50AA applies to SMFs (≥65% in debt/money-market) acquired on or after 01-04-2023. Under Sec 50AA, the gain is ALWAYS short-term irrespective of holding period and taxed at slab rates. Even though it was held ~5 years, the 5-year holding is irrelevant. A and B assume long-term treatment that Sec 50AA removes; D invents an exemption. The whole point of Sec 50AA is that these funds never get long-term/indexation benefit.
</details>

**Q4.** An individual investor holds a Sovereign Gold Bond (SGB) to its full 8-year maturity and receives the redemption amount. The redemption gain is:
A) Taxed as LTCG at 12.5%  B) Not chargeable to tax (Sec 47)  C) Taxed at slab rates  D) Taxed at 20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Section 47 provides that redemption of an SGB by an *individual* is not treated as a "transfer", so no capital gain arises. The interest (2.5% p.a.) is still taxable, but the capital appreciation on redemption is tax-free for individuals. A/C/D would apply only on a *secondary-market transfer* (a sale), or to a non-individual like a trust on redemption. The trap is forgetting that the exemption is only on redemption-by-individual, not on a sale.
</details>

**Q5.** Mr. X (resident) earns ₹5,00,000 dividend from a debt mutual fund and pays ₹1,50,000 interest on money borrowed to invest. What dividend is taxable?
A) ₹3,50,000  B) ₹5,00,000  C) ₹4,00,000  D) ₹3,80,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — A resident may deduct interest expense to earn MF dividend, but capped at 20% of the dividend. Cap = 5,00,000 × 20% = ₹1,00,000. Even though ₹1,50,000 was actually paid, only ₹1,00,000 is allowed. Taxable dividend = 5,00,000 − 1,00,000 = ₹4,00,000. A wrongly deducts the full ₹1,50,000; B forgets the deduction entirely; D uses a 24% figure. Formula: Taxable = Dividend − min(actual interest, 20% × dividend).
</details>

**Q6.** A T-Bill is issued at ₹97,800 and redeemed at face value ₹1,00,000 after 182 days. The ₹2,200 profit is:
A) Interest income, taxed at slab rates  B) STCG taxed at slab rates  C) LTCG at 12.5%  D) Exempt
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — T-Bills (maturity < 1 year) carry no coupon; the return is the difference between issue price and face value, and this profit is treated as a short-term capital gain taxed at the assessee's applicable rates. A is the common trap (treating discount as "interest") — the workbook classifies it as STCG, not interest. C needs >12-month listed status which is impossible for a T-bill. D invents an exemption.
</details>

**Q7.** A Gold ETF was bought on 01-Feb-2024 for ₹1,80,000 and sold on 01-Mar-2025 (after 1 April 2025) for ₹2,40,000. Holding period is more than 12 months. How is the ₹60,000 gain taxed?
A) STCG at slab rates  B) LTCG at 20%  C) Exempt under Sec 112A  D) LTCG at 12.5%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Gold ETFs are taxed like "other than equity-oriented" funds. The special "always-STCG" rule only applies to Gold ETFs *bought after 1-Apr-2023 AND sold before 1-Apr-2025*. Here the sale is on/after 1-Apr-2025, so the normal rule applies: held >12 months = LTCG at 12.5%. A would be right only if sold before 1-Apr-2025; B uses the wrong rate; C applies the equity-ETF section, but a Gold ETF is not equity-oriented.
</details>

**Q8.** ₹1,00,000 grows to ₹2,01,136 over 9 years. What is the CAGR (compound annual growth rate)?
A) 11.24% p.a.  B) 7.50% p.a.  C) 9.00% p.a.  D) 8.00% p.a.
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — CAGR = (End/Start)^(1/n) − 1, where n = number of years. = (2,01,136 / 1,00,000)^(1/9) − 1 = (2.01136)^(0.1111) − 1. Since 1.08^9 ≈ 2.0 (1.08^9 = 1.99900…≈2.011 with rounding), CAGR ≈ 8%. A is simple-return ÷ 9 = (101.136%/9 ≈ 11.24%), the classic "simple instead of compound" error. B and C are close decoys. Quick check: 1.08^9 = 1.999 ≈ 2.01.
</details>

**Q9.** You need ₹10,00,000 in 6 years. If you can earn 9% p.a. compounded annually, what lump sum must you invest today (PV)?
A) ₹5,96,267  B) ₹6,49,931  C) ₹5,46,000  D) ₹9,17,431
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — PV = FV / (1+r)^n = 10,00,000 / (1.09)^6. (1.09)^6 = 1.67710. PV = 10,00,000 / 1.67710 = ₹5,96,267. B uses (1.09)^5 wrongly; C uses simple-interest discounting; D discounts only one year (10,00,000/1.09). Each term: FV = target, r = 9%, n = 6 years.
</details>

**Q10.** Inflation is 6%. A nominal portfolio return is 11%. What is the approximate *real* return?
A) 5.00%  B) 17.66%  C) 4.72%  D) 4.50%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Real return = (1 + nominal)/(1 + inflation) − 1 = (1.11/1.06) − 1 = 1.047169 − 1 = 0.04717 = 4.72%. A is the rough subtraction (11% − 6%), which overstates the real return; B wrongly adds; D is a decoy. The exact formula matters: dividing by (1+inflation) is slightly less than simply subtracting.
</details>

**Q11.** A REIT distributes rental income to a unit-holder. In whose hands is this rental income taxed?
A) Exempt for everyone  B) Taxed in the unit-holder's hands (pass-through)  C) Taxed at the REIT level only  D) Taxed in both
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Rental income is exempt at the REIT level under Sec 10(23FCA) but is a *pass-through*: when distributed, it is taxed in the unit-holder's hands. Pass-through applies to (a) rental income, (c) dividend from SPV, (d) interest from SPV. A and C miss the pass-through mechanism; D double-counts. Key idea: pass-through means tax is "passed" to the investor, not paid twice.
</details>

**Q12.** For an InvIT, the rental income from property owned by the trust is taxed:
A) In the unit-holder's hands (pass-through)  B) Exempt entirely  C) At 12.5%  D) In the InvIT's own hands
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — InvITs are taxed like REITs EXCEPT for rental income: pass-through for rental income is given only to REITs. For an InvIT, rental income is taxed in the InvIT's own hands (income from business), not the unit-holder's. A is the REIT rule (the trap). B and C are wrong. This is the single key difference between REIT and InvIT taxation.
</details>

**Q13.** A high-value ULIP issued on 01-May-2022 has annual premium ₹3,00,000 and sum assured ₹40,00,000. On maturity, are the proceeds exempt under Sec 10(10D)?
A) Yes, fully exempt  B) No — premium exceeds 10% of sum assured  C) No — aggregate premium exceeds ₹2,50,000, so exemption is lost  D) Yes, because death benefit
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — For ULIPs issued on/after 1-Feb-2021, if the aggregate annual premium exceeds ₹2,50,000, the Sec 10(10D) exemption is NOT available and the gain is taxed as capital gains. Here ₹3,00,000 > ₹2,50,000, so exemption is lost. B is wrong because premium (₹3,00,000) is actually less than 10% of SA (₹4,00,000) — that test passes, but the ₹2.5L ULIP threshold is the binding one. A ignores the threshold; D applies only if the sum is received on death.
</details>

**Q14.** An endowment policy taken on 01-Apr-2024 has annual premium ₹6,00,000 and sum assured ₹70,00,000. The premium is well within 10% of SA. On maturity, the proceeds are:
A) Fully exempt under Sec 10(10D)  B) Taxed as LTCG at 12.5%  C) Exempt because SA > 10× premium  D) Taxable as income from other sources
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — For (non-ULIP) policies taken on/after 1-Apr-2023, if the premium (or aggregate premium) exceeds ₹5,00,000, the maturity proceeds are taxable as income from other sources even if SA is 10×+ of premium. Premium ₹6,00,000 > ₹5,00,000, so exemption is lost despite passing the 10%-of-SA test. A/C rely on tests that no longer override the ₹5L rule; B wrongly treats it as capital gains.
</details>

**Q15.** Mr. R retires and his total NPS corpus is ₹50,00,000. He withdraws the maximum tax-free lump sum allowed. How much can he withdraw tax-free?
A) ₹20,00,000  B) ₹30,00,000  C) ₹50,00,000  D) ₹25,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — On closure/opting out of NPS, withdrawal is exempt to the extent of 60% of the total corpus (in symmetry with the 60% lump-sum rule). 60% × 50,00,000 = ₹30,00,000 tax-free. The remaining 40% (₹20,00,000) must buy an annuity, whose pension is then taxable. A confuses lump-sum with the annuity portion; C assumes the whole corpus is tax-free; D uses 50%.
</details>

**Q17.** ₹50,000 invested today doubles in value. If the annual return is 12%, approximately how many years does it take (Rule of 72)?
A) 4 years  B) 8 years  C) 6 years  D) 12 years
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Q18.** A non-resident receives ₹2,00,000 dividend from an Indian debt mutual fund. At what rate is it taxed (ignore DTAA, surcharge, cess), and is any expense deductible?
A) Slab rates; 20% expense allowed  B) 12.5%; no expense  C) 10%; 20% expense  D) 20%; no expense deductible
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — For a non-resident, MF dividend is taxed at 20% (subject to DTAA), and NO expenditure (and no Chapter VI-A deduction) is allowed. So the full ₹2,00,000 is taxed at 20% = ₹40,000. A is the *resident* rule (slab + 20% expense cap); B and C use wrong rates. The non-resident loses the interest-expense deduction that residents get.
</details>

**Q19.** An investor SIPs ₹10,000 per month for 12 months earning 1% per month. Using the future value of an ordinary annuity, the maturity value is approximately:
A) ₹1,20,000  B) ₹1,26,825  C) ₹1,28,093  D) ₹1,21,200
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — FV of ordinary annuity = P × [((1+i)^n − 1)/i] = 10,000 × [((1.01)^12 − 1)/0.01]. (1.01)^12 = 1.126825, so ((1.126825 − 1)/0.01) = 12.6825. FV = 10,000 × 12.6825 = ₹1,26,825. A is just the principal (ignores growth); C wrongly uses the annuity-due formula; D applies simple interest. Terms: P = monthly amount, i = monthly rate, n = months.
</details>

**Q22.** For Category-I and Category-II AIFs, "other income" (non-business income) is:

**Q23.** A bond costing ₹1,000 has a yield-to-maturity of 8%. A single cash flow of ₹1,259.71 is due in 3 years. What is its present value at 8%?

**Q25.** An investor's portfolio: Year 0 value ₹2,00,000; Year 5 value ₹3,52,000. What is the CAGR?

**Correct: C)** — CAGR = (3,52,000/2,00,000)^(1/5) − 1 = (1.76)^(0.2) − 1. (1.76)^0.2 = e^(0.2 × ln1.76) = e^(0.2 × 0.56531) = e^0.113062 = 1.11970, so CAGR ≈ 11.97%.
